Insulin Therapy Lab

Three objectives, and they build on each other: know what each insulin does over time, know what each regimen trades away, and be able to look at a glucose pattern and say which insulin was acting when it went wrong. This page is built to be worked through rather than read.

What insulin therapy is trying to reproduce

Basal insulin restrains hepatic glucose output between meals and overnight; bolus insulin covers meals. Every regimen attempts to reproduce both jobs.

When a glucose pattern is abnormal, identify which insulin was acting beforehand.

The rule underneath everything

T1DM requires insulin at all times, with no exceptions for illness or reduced intake. Basal insulin is not "the insulin for food," and it is not paused because someone is not eating. Without any insulin at all, the liver resumes ketogenesis regardless of what the patient has or has not eaten.

RecognizeThe five categories, and what each one's shape means clinically

Insulin profiles

Know the categories and the examples within each. The curves let you see why the category matters clinically, not just as a label.

Rapid-acting
Lispro (Humalog), aspart (NovoLog), glulisine (Apidra). On and off quickly, with an early peak — which is what lets it match a meal and then get out of the way. It is also the only insulin used in a pump.
Regular — short-acting
Regular human insulin (R). Slower on, and it lasts longer than the meal does. That tail is the reason regimens using regular insulin traditionally require timed snacks: the insulin outlives the food.
NPH — intermediate
NPH (N). A broad mid-range peak. That peak is both its usefulness — it can cover a meal several hours after injection — and its hazard, because a peak that arrives while the patient is asleep is a peak nobody is watching.
Long-acting and ultra-long-acting
Glargine (Lantus), detemir (Levemir) — relatively flat across roughly a day. Degludec (Tresiba) — essentially peakless and lasting beyond 24 hours, so doses overlap between days and a change takes several days to show its full effect. Flatness is the feature: a basal insulin is meant to have no peak to fall off.
Premixed
70/30 and 75/25. A fixed ratio of an intermediate component and a rapid or short-acting component in one injection. Fewer injections, but the ratio is fixed — you cannot adjust the meal coverage without also moving the background insulin. That single constraint drives most of the trade-offs in the regimen table below.
A practical bedside detail worth knowing cold

NPH is cloudy. Regular and rapid-acting insulins are clear. It is a bedside safety check that costs nothing: if a vial that should be clear is cloudy, or a patient describes drawing up the wrong appearance, something has gone wrong before the injection.

Why the pump is described as the most physiological
It is the only regimen that can change the basal rate by time of day — more insulin in the pre-dawn hours when counter-regulatory hormones are rising, less during an afternoon of exercise. A basal injection commits you to one profile for the whole 24 hours. Temporary basal rates allow acute adjustments for illness or activity, and the fine dosing increments make ratios and correction factors usable in a way pens cannot match.
Why the pump's failure mode is the sharpest
A pump contains rapid-acting insulin only. There is no long-acting depot underneath it. When delivery stops — occluded set, dislodged cannula, empty reservoir — the patient's entire insulin supply stops within a couple of hours, and ketosis follows quickly. This is why unexplained hyperglycemia on a pump prompts a check of the delivery system before any thought of adjusting doses.

ActThe arithmetic, and then the pattern reading

The three numbers a patient carries

Insulin-to-carbohydrate ratio (ICR)
The insulin required to cover the breakdown of carbohydrate into sugars and keep glucose in target. Written as "1 unit per XX grams." A larger XX is a weaker ratio.
Correction factor (sensitivity factor)
The insulin required to bring an out-of-range glucose down to target. Written as "1 unit decreases glucose by XX mg/dL."
Basal insulin / basal rate, and target
The insulin required to hold glucose at target when fasting or not eating. The target glucose is the number the correction aims at — corrections are calculated from the distance above it, which is why two patients with the same glucose can need different corrections.

The patient whose correction factor is 1 unit per 80 mg/dL is far more sensitive to insulin — a single unit moves their glucose a long way, so they need very few units to correct. The patient at 1 unit per 25 is much more insulin-resistant; each unit accomplishes less, so they need several times as many units for the same correction.

Glucose pattern lab

The one principle this whole section teaches

Evaluate the insulin that acted before the repeated abnormal pattern.

Two words in that sentence do a lot of work. "Before" — you work backwards from the abnormal reading to whatever insulin was acting during the hours leading up to it, which is often not the insulin given nearest in time. "Repeated" — one bad day is a missed dose or an unusual meal, not a pattern. Confirm it repeats across several days before attributing it to an insulin period at all.

Working the same logic on a twice-daily NPH regimen

The reasoning is identical, but the answers change because the insulin shapes change. Read this table off the action curves rather than memorizing it — NPH given in the morning peaks in the afternoon, so it is the insulin covering the pre-dinner reading, and NPH given in the evening peaks overnight, so it is the insulin covering the fasting reading.

Why this table is a hazard as well as a tool

Look at the fasting row. On a twice-daily regimen, a high fasting glucose points to the evening NPH — but so does an overnight low, because it is the same insulin peaking at the same hour. Raising the evening NPH to fix a morning high, without first checking what happened at three in the morning, is precisely the error the dawn phenomenon versus Somogyi distinction exists to prevent.

Cases — reasoning across regimens

Original cases written to cover the regimen types above. Each opens to a worked answer.

A college student with T1DM takes a long-acting basal insulin each morning, with an insulin-to-carbohydrate ratio of 1 unit per 4 g and a correction factor of 1 unit per 20 mg/dL above 120. They report consistent morning highs and want to increase the basal dose. What do you want to know, and what is the risk of simply agreeing?

Ask what the glucose does overnight — the pattern between bedtime and waking, over several days. A steady drift upward supports increasing the basal. A dip in the early hours followed by a rebound means more basal would deepen a nocturnal hypoglycemia.

Then notice the timing. The basal is taken in the morning. Long-acting analogs are relatively flat but not perfectly so, and their weakest coverage tends to fall at the far end of the dosing interval — which here is the overnight period. So the question is not only how much basal but when it is given.

And check the bedtime bolus. A very strong ratio of 1 unit per 4 g means a large evening dose, and insulin still active from it changes the overnight picture. Look at the whole night before touching a standing dose.

A young adult with long-standing T1DM has switched from MDI to a premixed 70/30 regimen, taking a larger morning dose and a smaller evening dose, because the injection burden had become unmanageable. They now report lows in the mid-afternoon and highs before bed. What is happening, and what does the regimen make difficult?

The mid-afternoon low is the intermediate component of the morning injection peaking — a large morning dose means a large afternoon peak, arriving whether or not the patient eats. This is the reason twice-daily regimens require snacks timed to the insulin peaks.

The pre-bed high is the evening injection's rapid component being too small for the evening meal.

The bind: the 70/30 ratio is fixed. Reducing the morning dose to fix the afternoon low also reduces the morning meal coverage. Increasing the evening dose to cover dinner also increases the overnight intermediate component. You cannot move one component without moving the other — which is the trade the patient accepted in exchange for fewer injections. Recognizing that the regimen, not the patient, is generating the pattern is the clinical point.

An adult with T1DM on pump therapy with continuous glucose monitoring has held an A1c below 7.0% for years. It has risen to 8.1% since starting a new job. The pump is functioning and sets are being changed on schedule. Where do you look?

At the pattern, by time of day, and specifically at whether boluses are being given at all. A pump's great advantage — that a bolus is a few button presses rather than an injection — is also the easiest thing to postpone during a busy day and then forget. Missed or late mealtime boluses are among the most common reasons an established pump user's control drifts, and the bolus history in the pump answers the question directly.

Then ask what else changed with the job: meal timing, meal content, activity level, sleep, and stress. A regimen that worked for one daily rhythm may need different basal rates for a different one — which the pump can accommodate, once you know which hours are the problem.

Note what this case is not: the sets are being changed and the pump works, so this is not a delivery failure. Delivery failure looks like the pump-interruption trace in the pattern lab — a rapid, unrelenting rise over hours, not a gradual drift in A1c over months.

An older adult with long-standing T1DM and known hypoglycemia unawareness is on a twice-daily NPH and regular regimen. Why does that combination of patient and regimen deserve particular concern, and what changes if they move to MDI or a pump?

Two risks are being multiplied. NPH peaks, and a peak that arrives when the patient has not eaten on schedule causes hypoglycemia — which is why the regimen demands snacks timed to the peaks. Unawareness removes the adrenergic warning that would normally prompt the patient to treat it. The result is a predictable low, in a person who will not feel it coming, one of which lands overnight.

Moving to MDI replaces the peaking intermediate insulin with a relatively flat basal, which removes the scheduled afternoon and overnight peaks and decouples the insulin from meal timing. A pump goes further: basal rates can be lowered for the specific hours where lows cluster, and temporary basal rates handle exercise. Continuous glucose monitoring adds the piece unawareness took away — an alarm that notices the fall on the patient's behalf.

The general principle: when a patient cannot detect hypoglycemia, prefer regimens with fewer and flatter peaks and add external detection. This reasoning is why regimen choice is a clinical decision rather than a preference.
