Hypothalamus & Pituitary Disorders

One small gland, three ways to go wrong. Almost every disorder on this page is a variation on making too much of a hormone, making too little of it, or being a lump in a space that has no room for lumps — and a surprising number of patients have all three at once.

Pituitary anatomy and its hypothalamic connections

Where the gland sits, and why the anterior and posterior lobes fail in completely different ways.

The pituitary is about 1 cm across and weighs roughly 0.5 g. It sits at the base of the brain in the sella turcica, immediately below the optic chiasm, and hangs from the hypothalamus by a stalk that carries both blood vessels and axons. Those two things in the stalk — vessels and axons — are the whole reason the two lobes behave so differently.

Original diagram of the hypothalamic-pituitary anatomy and axes. The two routes out of the hypothalamus explain the clinical split: anterior lobe failure is a secretory problem in the pituitary itself, whereas posterior lobe failure can originate anywhere along the hypothalamus–stalk–posterior lobe path.

Anterior lobe — histology
Five functional cell types: somatotropes (GH), lactotropes (prolactin), corticotropes (ACTH), gonadotropes (FSH/LH), and thyrotropes (TSH).
Posterior lobe — histology
Axons, Herring bodies, and pituicytes rather than glandular cells. It stores and releases hormones made in the hypothalamus.
Primary, secondary, tertiary — define it once and reuse it everywhere

This framework applies to every axis, so it is worth over-learning. Primary: the end organ has failed — the pituitary or hypothalamic hormone is elevated while the end-organ hormone is low. Secondary: the pituitary has failed — pituitary hormone low, hypothalamic hormone elevated, end-organ hormone low. Tertiary: the hypothalamus has failed — all three low. Secondary and tertiary together are called central.

Primary — the adrenal cortex has failed, so ACTH is high as the pituitary tries to flog a gland that cannot respond. Secondary — the pituitary has failed, so ACTH is low and cortisol is low; CRH from the hypothalamus would be elevated. Tertiary — the hypothalamus has failed, so CRH, ACTH, and cortisol are all low. The trick that makes exam questions answerable: look at whether the upstream hormone moved in the same direction as the downstream one, or the opposite.

Mass effect and the optic chiasm

Why a lesion in the sella takes the outer half of each visual field, and nothing else.

Three consequences of local mass effect: headache from raised intracranial pressure, an abnormal sella turcica on imaging, and visual field loss, most commonly bitemporal hemianopsia. The last one is worth understanding rather than memorizing, because the anatomy explains the exact field defect.

Original diagram. The chain to be able to say out loud: temporal field → nasal retina → crossing fibers → sit in the middle of the chiasm → a mass pushing up from the sella hits the middle first → both temporal fields go. "Bitemporal" describes the field that is lost, not the retina.

Distinguish from

A defect in one eye is a lesion of that optic nerve, in front of the chiasm. A homonymous defect — the same side of the field lost in both eyes — is a lesion behind the chiasm, in the tract or radiation. Only a lesion at the chiasm produces the bitemporal pattern.

Hypopituitarism and the order hormones are lost

Hypopituitarism is loss of anterior pituitary output, from any cause. Two points carry most of the exam weight: GH is deficient in most patients with hypopituitarism, and acquired hypopituitarism tends to follow a sequential order of loss.

Original diagram of the classically taught sequence. The ordering is a tendency, not a law — any individual can lose axes out of order, particularly after surgery, radiation, or apoplexy.

What each anterior deficiency looks like. Note that the pituitary hormone and the end-organ hormone are both low — that is the signature of central disease.

Clinical definition
Isolated GH deficiency is a defined group of conditions involving absent GH action and severe short stature.

The named structural and vascular lesions

Six entities the objectives name individually. Each card opens; each one ends with a distinguishing feature worth having ready.

Rathke pouch remnant, classically in a child with growth failure, hypopituitarism, headache, and visual-field loss from a suprasellar mass.

Anterior pituitary infarction after severe postpartum hemorrhage → failure to lactate, amenorrhea, fatigue, and other anterior hormone deficiencies.

The time-sensitive one on this page

Acute hemorrhage into a pituitary adenoma with rapid enlargement. It causes an excruciating headache, vision changes, and hypopituitarism — and it can cause cardiovascular collapse and sudden death. It is a true neurosurgical emergency. The circulatory collapse is the acute loss of ACTH and therefore cortisol.

The pituitary is shrunken or flattened and the sella then fills with cerebrospinal fluid, so on imaging the sella looks empty. The clinically important part: with a partial empty sella, pituitary function is often normal. An "empty sella" on a scan is therefore not automatically a diagnosis of hypopituitarism — it is a reason to test the axes, not to assume them.

Optic nerve hypoplasia + absent septum pellucidum + pituitary hormone deficiency. Midline defects or neonatal hypoglycemia should prompt evaluation for congenital hypopituitarism.

Benign Rathke pouch remnant cyst, often incidental but capable of causing anterior pituitary deficiency by local compression.

Growth hormone deficiency

The axis
GH acts largely through IGF-1, generated principally in the liver. That is why the workup measures both: GH is pulsatile and a single random level is close to uninterpretable, whereas IGF-1 integrates GH exposure over time.
What suppresses GH secretion
Malnutrition · illness · hyperglycemia. The last one is the mechanistic basis of the diagnostic test for GH excess.
What stimulates GH secretion
Deep sleep · stress · exercise · hypoglycemia · sex steroids. Again, the last one is the basis of a diagnostic test — this time for GH deficiency.
Testing
GH stimulation tests are used, because a low random GH proves nothing. In the insulin tolerance test, insulin is given to induce hypoglycemia; someone with adequate GH reserve raises their GH in response. Failure to rise is the abnormal result.
Adult GH deficiency is not just a height problem

Adults have a genuinely diverse clinical picture: visceral adiposity, low bone mass, increased cardiovascular disease, excessive tiredness, anxiety, depression, reduced quality of life, and possibly premature death. In children the presentation is dominated by short stature.

Serum GH rises. Insulin drives the glucose down; hypoglycemia is a physiological stimulus to GH secretion, so a healthy somatotroph population responds. The test works by provoking the axis rather than sampling it — which is necessary because GH is secreted in pulses and a low random value is uninformative. A failure to rise is what defines the deficiency.

Size vocabulary
Microadenoma < 1 cm · macroadenoma > 1 cm. Larger lesions can erode the sella, compress the chiasm, and cause hypopituitarism.
Pathology — the two headline findings
Cellular monomorphism: a pure population of acidophils, basophils, or chromophobes, rather than the mixed population of the normal gland. Loss of the reticulin network on a reticulin (silver) stain — the normal acinar architecture breaks down. Those two together are how an adenoma is distinguished from hyperplasia.
Pituitary carcinoma

Extremely rare. It is distinguished from an adenoma by craniospinal or systemic metastasis, not by local invasion or microscopic appearance alone.

Prolactin regulation and prolactinoma

Prolactin is the odd one out: it is the only anterior pituitary hormone under tonic inhibition. Understand that one fact and four different diseases fall out of it.

Original diagram. The gray dashed line ending in a bar is this site's convention for inhibition or a lost signal — it is used the same way on every page.

Four routes to a high prolactin, and one route back down. Reading the middle column is usually enough to answer the question.
SituationWhat happens to the dopamine brakeClinical fingerprint
PhysiologicNormal regulation, appropriately overriddenPregnancy, suckling or nipple stimulation, stress. Not a disease.

Mechanism
Autonomous prolactin secretion from a lactotroph adenoma. Serum prolactin correlates positively with the size of the adenoma.
Presentation
Amenorrhea, galactorrhea, loss of libido, infertility, delayed puberty. Typically ages 20–40. The diagnosis is made more readily in females, because menstrual disturbance and galactorrhea declare themselves earlier than a slowly falling libido — so men more often present late, with mass effect.
Pathology
Dystrophic calcification, which when extensive is called a "pituitary stone." Prolactin immunohistochemistry confirms the lineage; reticulin stain shows the loss of acinar architecture common to all adenomas.
Management
Medication first — bromocriptine or cabergoline, dopamine receptor agonists that restore inhibition of prolactin release. Surgery is second-line. This is unusual among pituitary tumors, where surgery is more often the primary answer.
Course emphasis

Two things carry most of the exam weight: prolactinoma is the most common functional adenoma, and it is the one where drugs beat surgery. Add the size–prolactin correlation and you can separate a true prolactinoma from stalk effect.

Growth hormone excess — acromegaly and gigantism

Same hormone, same tumor, two entirely different bodies. The only variable that matters is whether the growth plates are still open.

Original diagram. If a question gives you GH excess and asks which phenotype, the only thing you need from the stem is the age at onset.

Acromegaly — the face and hands
Protrusion of the lower jaw (prognathism), prominent supraorbital ridges, broadening of the nose, thickening of the lips, and thickened fingers. Old photographs are genuinely diagnostic, because the change is slow enough that nobody around the patient notices.
Acromegaly — the systemic cost
GH excess also causes gonadal dysfunction, diabetes mellitus, hypertension, arthritis, congestive heart failure, and an increased risk of cancers. The cardiovascular and metabolic burden, not the appearance, is what shortens life.
Diagnosis
Elevated serum GH and IGF-1. Then the confirmatory test: failure to suppress GH in response to an oral glucose tolerance test, one of the most sensitive tests for the diagnosis. It works because hyperglycemia normally suppresses GH — a tumor ignores that. Imaging with MRI, plus bone scan.
Treatment
Surgery, then drug therapy: somatostatin analogs (somatostatin is GHIH, growth-hormone-inhibiting hormone) or a GH receptor antagonist. Note the contrast with prolactinoma, where medication comes first.

Hyperglycemia normally suppresses GH secretion. Load someone with glucose and a healthy somatotroph population obediently shuts down. An autonomous somatotroph adenoma does not — so the confirmatory finding is failure of GH to suppress after the oral glucose load. It is the mirror image of the insulin tolerance test used for GH deficiency, where you provoke with hypoglycemia and look for a rise.

The remaining functional adenomas

Cushing disease — meaning specifically the pituitary cause of Cushing syndrome — is oversecretion of ACTH from a corticotroph adenoma, stimulating the adrenals to overproduce cortisol. On histology, corticotroph tumors show Crooke hyaline change.

Nelson syndrome — follow the feedback

A large, destructive corticotroph adenoma appearing after bilateral adrenalectomy performed for Cushing disease. Removing the adrenals removes the inhibitory effect of adrenal corticosteroids on the tumor, which is then free to grow. The giveaway is the combination: hypercortisolism is absent — the adrenals are gone — yet the patient has mass effect and hyperpigmentation, the latter driven by the very high ACTH.

Scope

The adrenal side of Cushing syndrome, including the diagnostic workup separating pituitary from adrenal from ectopic sources, belongs to a different session. This page stays at the depth this topic needs.

Usually found in middle-aged men and women presenting with neurologic symptoms once the adenoma is large enough — which is the paradox of this tumor: it secretes hormone, but the hormone it secretes does not produce a florid syndrome, so it behaves clinically much like a non-functioning tumor. It causes decreased libido and amenorrhea in premenopausal women, and secondary gonadal hypofunction, usually through impaired LH even though FSH is the predominant secreted hormone. Histology shows pseudorosettes around vascular channels and oncocytic change.

An extremely rare cause of hyperthyroidism. It earns its place here entirely because of its lab pattern: elevated TSH and elevated T4 and T3.

Why that pattern is the whole point

Every other hyperthyroid state has a suppressed TSH, because excess thyroid hormone feeds back and shuts the pituitary down. A TSH that is high while the thyroid hormones are also high means the pituitary is driving the process rather than responding to it. When you see both elevated, the lesion is upstream. Posterior pituitary — the three water disorders

One hormone, and the three ways it goes wrong: too much, not enough, or present but ignored.

Mechanism
AVP excess causes reabsorption of excessive free water. Retaining water without retaining solute dilutes the serum — hence hyponatremia.
Presentation
Neurologic, and driven by the rate of the sodium fall: confusion, cerebral edema, and neurologic dysfunction including seizures.
Diagnostic pattern
↓ serum osmolality and ↓ sodium, with ↑ urine osmolality and ↑ specific gravity. Urine output is low despite appropriate or increased hydration — the kidney is concentrating when it should be dumping water.
Management
Water restriction.
The finding that surprises people

Total body water is increased, yet peripheral edema does not develop, because blood volume stays normal — the retained water distributes across all compartments rather than pooling in the interstitium. A hyponatremic patient with pitting edema is telling you about heart, liver, or kidney failure instead.

Mechanism
Deficient AVP secretion. Because AVP is made in the hypothalamus, the lesion can sit anywhere along the hypothalamus–stalk–posterior lobe path.
Causes
Head trauma, tumors, surgery, and inflammation of the hypothalamus and pituitary gland.
Presentation
Polyuria and polydipsia. Large volumes of dilute urine with low specific gravity and low urine osmolality, and a concentrated serum with a raised sodium and osmolality.
Treatment
Replace the missing hormone — desmopressin (DDAVP).
Mechanism
Renal tubular unresponsiveness to circulating AVP. The hormone is there; the collecting duct will not listen.
Causes
Medications — lithium is the most commonly cited cause. Congenital disease from an X-linked mutation of the AVPR2 gene, which encodes the receptor.
Presentation
Clinically almost identical to AVP-D. Polyuria, polydipsia, dilute urine, concentrated serum. You cannot tell them apart at the bedside.
So how do you separate them?
By responsiveness to administered AVP and by measurement of serum AVP. That is the entire content of the next section.
The danger in both AVP disorders

Patients with an intact thirst mechanism and free access to water can generally compensate for the urinary losses, and look deceptively well. Remove either of those — an infant, someone with altered consciousness, a patient made nil by mouth for surgery, anyone with hypothalamic damage that also took out thirst — and they develop life-threatening dehydration. The polyuria is not the emergency; the inability to keep up with it is.

Water deprivation, then desmopressin

  1. Water deprivation: continued high-volume dilute urine confirms failure to concentrate.
  2. Desmopressin: urine osmolality rises in AVP deficiency but changes little in AVP resistance.
Safety — this is a supervised test, not a bedside maneuver

Water deprivation can cause dangerous dehydration and hypernatremia and must be performed under close supervision.

Drugs named in this session

Bromocriptine, cabergoline
Dopamine receptor agonists. They restore the tonic inhibition of prolactin release that the disease removed — which is why they work on prolactinoma and why they are first-line ahead of surgery.
Desmopressin (DDAVP)
Synthetic arginine vasopressin. Both the treatment for AVP deficiency and the discriminating step in the diagnostic test — the same molecule doing two jobs.
Somatostatin analogs
Somatostatin is GHIH, growth-hormone-inhibiting hormone. The analogs suppress GH secretion in acromegaly when surgery is not the answer or has not been enough.
Drugs that cause these problems
D2-receptor antagonists (phenothiazines, haloperidol) raise prolactin. Lithium causes AVP resistance. On any endocrine vignette, read the medication list before the labs.
