T2DM

T1DM is an event. T2DM is a trajectory: insulin resistance → compensatory hyperinsulinemia → beta-cell dysfunction → worsening hyperglycemia. Almost every clinical decision on this page — when to screen, why to intervene early, why complications are present at diagnosis — follows from where on that curve the patient is standing.

Insulin resistance, compensation, and beta-cell failure

Insulin resistance
Target tissues — muscle, fat, liver — respond poorly to insulin. Glucose is not taken up efficiently and the liver does not suppress its output as it should. Nothing is wrong with the beta cell yet.
Compensatory hyperinsulinemia
The beta cell answers resistance by secreting more insulin, and for a long while it succeeds: glucose stays normal at the cost of a rising insulin level. This is why a normal glucose does not mean a normal metabolism — and why C-peptide is high, not low, in T2DM.
Beta-cell dysfunction
The compensation is not sustainable. Secretory capacity declines, and the gap between what is needed and what is delivered opens. Insulin deficiency in T2DM is relative, not absolute — which is exactly why ketoacidosis is uncommon here and hyperosmolar states are not.
Worsening hyperglycemia
Glucose crosses the prediabetes range, then the diabetes threshold. By this point the process has been running for years — which is why microvascular complications are sometimes already present at the moment of diagnosis, and why screening at-risk people rather than waiting for symptoms matters so much.
Genetics and environment

T2DM has a strong genetic contribution, expressed through interaction with nutrition, activity, sleep, and other environmental factors.

RecognizeWho to suspect, what to look for, and what the labs show

Clinical suspicion and physical findings

Symptoms
Polyuria, polydipsia, polyphagia — the same three Ps, because the mechanism downstream of the hyperglycemia is identical. Plus nocturia and nocturnal enuresis. The difference from T1DM is tempo: this often accumulates so gradually that the patient does not report it, and the diagnosis comes from a screening test rather than a complaint.
Signs and history
Acanthosis nigricans · overweight or obesity · family history, which carries particular weight given how strong the heritability is here. Look at the back of the neck and the axillae on anybody you are screening.
Acanthosis nigricans — what it actually is

Velvety, thickened, hyperpigmented skin in flexural areas: the back and sides of the neck, the axillae, the groin, sometimes the knuckles. It is not a hygiene problem and does not scrub off — a distinction worth making explicitly, because patients are frequently told otherwise. It is a visible marker of insulin resistance, and a recognized clinical sign of both prediabetes and T2DM. Finding it on examination should prompt a glucose or A1c, not reassurance.

Screening, prediabetes, and the diagnostic thresholds

Original figure. The conventional written forms of the same cut-points are fasting ≥126 mg/dL, 2-hour ≥200 mg/dL, and A1c ≥6.5% — the strict-inequality phrasing above describes the same boundaries. Bar widths are for legibility and are not to scale.

Prediabetes / impaired glucose tolerance
Defined by the middle band on all three tests. Acanthosis nigricans is a clinical sign at this stage too, and treatment is nutrition and exercise, with medication therapy as an option. The framing that matters: prediabetes is not a mild version of diabetes, it is a point on the curve where the trajectory can still be changed.
Labs that support T2DM specifically
Negative GAD-65 antibody, islet cell antibody, and insulin autoantibody. Elevated C-peptide and insulin. Read together: no autoimmunity, and plenty of endogenous insulin being made — the problem is that it is not working. Compare with T1DM, where C-peptide is low.

Metabolic syndrome

Also called insulin resistance syndrome, or syndrome X — named in Gerald Reaven's 1988 Banting Lecture as a cluster of effects downstream of hyperinsulinemia and insulin resistance.

Original diagram of the pathway Reaven's framework describes. Note the causal direction: abdominal obesity drives insulin resistance, resistance produces hyperinsulinemia, and the cluster of features radiates from there rather than merely co-occurring.

Reaven GM. Banting Lecture 1988. Role of insulin resistance in human disease. Diabetes. 1988;37(12):1595–1607. doi:10.2337/diab.37.12.1595

What insulin resistance produces
Impaired peripheral glucose and fatty acid utilization · vascular endothelial dysfunction · impaired lipid metabolism · hypertension. Note that endothelial dysfunction appears here, at the metabolic-syndrome stage — before diabetes is diagnosed. The vascular disease does not wait for the glucose threshold.
The detail that catches people
Explicitly: BMI and weight are not a criterion — it is abdominal obesity that matters. Someone with a BMI in the normal range but central adiposity can meet the definition, and someone with a high BMI and a favorable fat distribution may not.
The comorbidities that travel with T2DM
Hypertension · dyslipidemia · fatty liver disease · chronic kidney disease · polycystic ovary syndrome · obstructive sleep apnea · hyperuricemia and gout · metabolic syndrome. Most share insulin resistance as a common root, which is why they cluster.

Hyperosmolar hyperglycemic state, and how it differs from DKA

Diagnostic features

Hyperglycemia — glucose above 600 mg/dL. Hyperosmolality — serum osmolality above 330 mOsm/kg. No acidosis, and the absence of significant ketosis. Those three together define it.

Kitabchi AE, Umpierrez GE, Miles JM, Fisher JN. Hyperglycemic crises in adult patients with diabetes. Diabetes Care. 2009;32(7):1335–1343. doi:10.2337/dc09-9032

Why there are no ketones — the mechanistic heart of the topic

HHS arises from relative insulin deficiency, with a rise in counter-regulatory hormones because glucose is not being used, and increased hepatic and renal glucose production through gluconeogenesis. The idea is worth memorizing almost verbatim: there is just enough circulating insulin to prevent lipolysis and the subsequent free fatty acid production, but not enough insulin to support adequate glucose utilization.

Say it as a threshold problem

Suppressing lipolysis takes less insulin than driving glucose into cells does. So as insulin availability falls, glucose uptake fails first while lipolysis stays switched off. The patient becomes profoundly hyperglycemic without making ketones. Only when insulin falls further — as in T1DM — does lipolysis get released and ketogenesis begin. One axis, two thresholds: that single idea explains why DKA and HHS are different presentations of the same hormone deficit, and why the type of diabetes predicts which one you tend to see without determining it.

Patients with T2DM can present with DKA, and older adults with T1DM can develop HHS. The associations in the last two rows above are typical, not defining. Diagnose the emergency from the glucose, the ketones, the acid–base status, and the osmolality — never from the label already in the chart.

HHS complications
Thrombosis — profound dehydration and hyperviscosity · rhabdomyolysis, and through it acute kidney injury, hyperkalemia, and hypocalcemia, which together can cause cardiac arrest · muscle swelling leading to compartment syndrome · malignant hyperthermia · cerebral edema.

Treatment

The point of this section is that there are many treatment modalities — the emphasis is on the categories and the lifestyle foundation, not on individual drug pharmacology.

Nutrition and dietary counseling
Weight stabilization, then weight loss — a widely used initial target is 7% of body weight · eliminate sugary beverages · encourage plant-based eating · and, importantly, identify the patient's dietary preferences. Named patterns: Mediterranean, low calorie, low fat, DASH, intermittent fasting. Medical nutrition therapy centers on no concentrated sugars and carbohydrate monitoring, with support from family, friends, school, and work.
Exercise counseling
Children: 60 minutes of moderate-to-vigorous aerobic activity per day. Adults: 150 minutes per week. Muscle- and bone-strengthening activity on 3 days per week for both. Screen-time counseling belongs alongside these, particularly for children and adolescents.
Glucose monitoring
Patient-specific. Options: fasting, pre-meal, and 2-hour post-prandial checks; standard basal-bolus monitoring at 4 times daily; or continuous glucose monitoring.
Medication categories
Four broad modalities: oral medication, insulin therapy, oral medication plus insulin, and injectable glucose-lowering agents other than insulin. Insulin in T2DM is a tool used when the others are insufficient, not a sign of failure — a framing worth passing on to patients.

A scope note on pharmacology

This page stays at the level of treatment categories. It does not work through the mechanisms, adverse effects, or comparative benefits of individual agents — metformin, sulfonylureas, SGLT2 inhibitors, GLP-1 receptor agonists, DPP-4 inhibitors, and the rest. Coursework cases mention specific drugs by name, so recognizing which category a named drug belongs to is reasonable to expect; detailed pharmacology is not covered here and this page does not manufacture it. One safety-relevant point does belong here, because it appears in the practice bank: among oral agents, the insulin secretagogues — the sulfonylureas, such as glipizide, glyburide, and glimepiride — can cause hypoglycemia, particularly when meals are missed or delayed, whereas metformin used alone does not.

Microvascular complications

Retinopathy
Retinal ischemia and neovascularization; fragile new vessels can bleed and threaten vision.
Nephropathy
Albuminuria/proteinuria from glomerular injury; control glucose and blood pressure and screen for early disease.
Neuropathy
Sorbitol accumulation and AGE injury damage peripheral nerves and Schwann cells.
Macrovascular complications
Coronary artery, cerebrovascular, and peripheral arterial disease from endothelial dysfunction and accelerated atherosclerosis.
