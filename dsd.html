DSD Concepts

Sexual development happens in three sequential steps — chromosomal, then gonadal, then phenotypic — and a difference of sex development is a break at one of them. Learn the normal sequence properly and the diagnoses sort themselves: the karyotype tells you which step to interrogate, the gonads tell you whether the second step worked, and the external genitalia tell you what hormones the fetus actually saw. One of these diagnoses can kill an infant in the second week of life, so the evaluation is urgent as well as careful.

A note on the words

DSD stands for disorders of sex development in the 2006 Chicago consensus statement that introduced it; differences of sex development is now widely preferred, and many affected people use intersex variation. The consensus replaced intersex, hermaphrodite, and pseudohermaphrodite, which are stigmatizing and should not be used clinically, and grounded the classification in karyotype instead of appearance. You will still meet the old words in older texts and in pathology reports. Sex and gender are not the same thing: this page is about the biology of sex development, and it does not predict gender identity.

Three steps, in order

Original diagram. Read the three horizontal bands as three independent questions, because that is how the diseases behave. Chromosomal: what karyotype? Gonadal: did a testis form? Phenotypic: were AMH, testosterone, and DHT each delivered and each received? Almost every diagnosis on this page is a single one of those signals failing while the others work — which is why the phenotypes are so specific.

Chromosomal sex
Determined at conception. The determining element is SRY — the sex-determining region on the short arm of the Y chromosome — which acts as a switch that turns on the Sertoli cell programme. Many other transcription factors participate; SRY is the trigger, not the whole mechanism.
Gonadal sex
The bipotential gonad differentiates at around six weeks post-conception (the second month). With SRY it becomes a testis; without SRY it becomes an ovary. Everything downstream depends on which one formed, because the phenotype is driven by hormones the gonad makes.
Phenotypic sex
Driven by the gonad's output. Internal ducts: both Müllerian and Wolffian systems are present initially and one set regresses. External genitalia: a single bipotential anlage that virilizes if it sees enough DHT. The timing and pattern vary, and that variation is diagnostically useful.
The three hormonal signals, and what each one does

AMH, from Sertoli cells — regresses the Müllerian ducts, which would otherwise become the uterus, fallopian tubes, and upper two-thirds of the vagina. Acts locally, so a unilateral testis regresses only its own side. Testosterone, from Leydig cells — maintains the Wolffian ducts (epididymis, vas deferens, seminal vesicles). Also acts locally and at high concentration, which is why circulating adrenal androgen cannot substitute for it. DHT, converted from testosterone by 5α-reductase in the target tissue — is the potent virilizer of the external genitalia. Testosterone alone will not do this job, and that single fact explains an entire disease.

Timing decides what the virilization looks like

In a 46,XX fetus, androgen exposure before 13 weeks of gestation — while the labioscrotal folds are still able to fuse — produces posterior labial fusion and a urogenital sinus. Exposure from about 14 weeks onward, once those structures have already formed, produces clitoromegaly without fusion. So the degree and type of virilization tells you when the androgen arrived, which narrows the differential before any test comes back.

Why a DSD is both a medical and a psychosocial emergency

Objective 1. There are two independent reasons, and they need different responses.

The medical emergency
Some of these infants are in the first days of a life-threatening endocrine illness. Congenital adrenal hyperplasia — the commonest cause of a 46,XX DSD — produces adrenal crisis at roughly 10 to 21 days of life: vomiting, poor feeding, failure to thrive, dehydration, hyponatremia, hyperkalemia, hypoglycemia, and shock. The infant looks well at birth. Panhypopituitarism is the other one to fear, presenting with hypoglycemia and micropenis. Both are treatable and both are lethal untreated, which is what makes the evaluation urgent rather than merely thorough.
The psychosocial emergency
"Is it a boy or a girl?" is the first question anyone asks about a newborn, and the family cannot answer it. The consequences are real: acute distress and anxiety in the parents, disruption of bonding with the infant, pressure from relatives, and decisions made under time pressure that are irreversible. The response is not speed for its own sake — it is rapid, coordinated evaluation with honest ongoing communication, so that the family is never left without information and never pushed into an early decision.
How the team communicates while the workup runs

Use neutral anatomical language — clitorophallus, labioscrotal folds, gonads — rather than sex-specific terms, and avoid pronouns until sex of rearing is agreed. Do not guess, and do not offer a provisional answer that may have to be retracted. Involve the parents in an informed discussion from the beginning rather than presenting them with conclusions. And the team must speak with one consensus voice: contradictory statements from different specialists are one of the most damaging things that can happen to a family in this situation.

Scale

DSDs occur in roughly 1 in 4,500 to 5,500 live births taken together — uncommon individually, and collectively something any pediatrician or family physician will meet.

The findings that warrant evaluation

Objective 2. Note that several of these occur in an infant whose genitalia look unremarkable at a glance.

External genital findings
Apparent female: clitoromegaly, posterior labial fusion, a single perineal opening (urogenital sinus), or an inguinal or labial mass, which may be a testis. Apparent male: bilateral non-palpable gonads, severe hypospadias — particularly perineal or penoscrotal — hypospadias with undescended testis, micropenis, and a bifid scrotum. Frankly ambiguous genitalia in either direction.
Findings away from the genitalia
Signs of adrenal insufficiency — poor feeding, vomiting, failure to thrive, dehydration, hypotension, hyperpigmentation of the genital skin and areolae, hypoglycemia, hyponatremia with hyperkalemia. Associated congenital anomalies, since several DSDs are syndromic. A discordance between prenatal karyotype and the appearance at birth. And a family history of DSD, consanguinity, unexplained neonatal death, or infertility.
One physical sign carries more weight than any other

A palpable gonad is almost always a testis. Ovaries do not descend. So a palpable gonad in the labioscrotal fold or the inguinal canal means testicular tissue is present, which immediately makes a 46,XX DSD unlikely and reorients the workup toward undervirilization of a 46,XY infant, ovotesticular DSD, or gonadal dysgenesis. Symmetry matters too: symmetric findings suggest a hormonal cause, asymmetric findings suggest dysgenesis. Bilaterally non-palpable gonads in an apparently male infant should be treated as CAH until excluded — that infant may be a virilized 46,XX girl heading for a salt-wasting crisis.

The workup

Examination, documented precisely
Phallic length and diameter. Labioscrotal folds — fused or not, and how far posteriorly. Position of the urethral meatus. Anogenital ratio. Palpable gonads — present or absent, and on which side. Hyperpigmentation, which points to high ACTH and therefore to CAH. Plus a general examination for dysmorphic features and other anomalies.
First-line laboratory studies
Karyotype — with FISH for SRY where relevant — as the first branch point. 17-hydroxyprogesterone, because CAH is the diagnosis that cannot wait. Serum electrolytes and glucose, followed serially through the first two weeks. Testosterone, DHT, AMH, LH and FSH, and further steroid precursors as the pattern directs.
Imaging
Pelvic and abdominal ultrasound first: is there a uterus, are there intra-abdominal or streak gonads, is there adrenal hyperplasia. Genitogram to define a urogenital sinus and the anatomy of the vagina and urethra. MRI or CT of the abdomen and pelvis when the ultrasound leaves questions open.
Who is on the team

A coordinated group, assembled at once rather than sequentially: neonatology or general pediatrics, endocrinology, urology, pediatric surgery, gynecology, genetics, and psychology or mental health. Social work and ethics support where available. The team's most important operational rule is the one already stated — speak with one voice.

46,XX DSD — the over-virilized

Objective 3. Ovaries, a uterus, and a fetus that saw androgen it should not have seen.

Pathophysiology
Autosomal recessive; an inborn error of cortisol and aldosterone production, over 90% of cases from 21-hydroxylase deficiency. Low cortisol removes feedback, ACTH rises, the adrenals become hyperplastic, and the accumulated precursor is shunted into the androgen branch. The full derivation is on the steroid synthesis page.
Presentation
A 46,XX infant with atypical genitalia — clitoromegaly, labioscrotal fusion, a urogenital sinus — and no palpable gonads, because the ovaries are intra-abdominal. Internal structures (uterus, tubes, ovaries) are normal. Then, in the salt-wasting form, adrenal crisis at 10–21 days: failure to thrive, progressive weight loss, dehydration, high potassium, low sodium, low cortisol, shock. Hyperpigmentation of the genital skin is a useful early clue.
Evaluation
17-hydroxyprogesterone — markedly raised. Karyotype 46,XX. Ultrasound shows a uterus and often hyperplastic adrenals. Electrolytes and glucose, repeated: the crisis develops over days and a normal sodium on day 2 means nothing on day 12.
Why this one is first on every list
It is both the commonest cause of a 46,XX DSD and the only cause of atypical genitalia that is immediately life-threatening. Every newborn genital-anomaly workup is built around excluding it first. Frequency is usually quoted at roughly 1 in 15,000 births for the classic form.
Pathophysiology
Aromatase normally converts fetal androgens to estrogens in the placenta, protecting both fetus and pregnant person from androgen exposure. In its absence — biallelic CYP19A1 variants, autosomal recessive — those androgens cross unchanged.
Presentation
Both the baby and the pregnant person virilize. Maternal acne, hirsutism, and voice change appear during the pregnancy, often from the second trimester, and regress after delivery. The 46,XX infant has atypical genitalia ranging from clitoromegaly to complete labioscrotal fusion. Later: absent puberty with tall stature, ovarian cysts, delayed bone age and low bone density from estrogen deficiency.
How it is distinguished from CAH
Maternal virilization is the discriminator, because in CAH the mother is unaffected. Biochemically, 17-OHP is normal, and estrogens are low with high androgens. A maternal luteoma or exogenous androgen exposure produces a similar fetal picture and is excluded by history.
Pathophysiology
During paternal meiosis, SRY translocates onto an X chromosome. The resulting 46,XX embryo receives the testis-determining signal, so the bipotential gonad becomes a testis and the phenotype develops along male lines. About 80% of nonsyndromic 46,XX testicular DSD is SRY-positive; the remainder involve other genes in the same pathway.
Presentation
Usually a phenotypic male, sometimes with hypospadias or small testes. Many are not identified until adolescence or adult infertility work-up. Because the rest of the Y — including the azoospermia factor region — is absent, there is azoospermia and infertility, and testosterone often falls short at puberty with a compensatory rise in gonadotropins.
Evaluation
Karyotype 46,XX with a FISH probe positive for SRY (or chromosomal microarray). Incidence is on the order of 1 in 20,000. Long-term care is testosterone replacement if needed, and fertility counselling.

46,XY DSD — the under-virilized

Objective 4. A testis formed. Something after that failed — production, conversion, or reception.

Sort them by which of the three signals failed

AMH failed → Müllerian structures persist in an otherwise male infant. Testosterone production failed → both duct systems and the external genitalia are underdeveloped. Conversion to DHT failed → internal male structures are present, external genitalia are not virilized. Reception failed → nothing androgen-dependent forms at all, but AMH still worked, so there is no uterus. That last contrast — no androgen effect with no Müllerian structures — is the fingerprint of androgen insensitivity.

Pathophysiology
Absent AMH production or a defective AMH receptor. The Sertoli cells fail to deliver the regression signal, so the Müllerian ducts persist. Leydig cell function is normal, so testosterone is made and the Wolffian structures are also present — this is the one condition where both duct systems coexist.
Presentation
Phenotypically male external genitalia, with undescended testes — the Müllerian remnants tether them. Inguinal hernia is common, and the diagnosis is classically made at hernia repair or orchiopexy when a uterus and tubes are found unexpectedly.
Management
Orchiopexy to retrieve the testes and place them in the scrotum, with careful handling of the Müllerian remnants and the vas, which run close together. Long-term concerns are fertility and the malignancy risk that accompanies cryptorchidism.
Pathophysiology
A defect can sit at almost every step of the pathway from cholesterol to testosterone — StAR, CYP11A1, 3β-HSD, CYP17A1, 17β-HSD — or in the Leydig cell itself, through hypoplasia or an inactivating LH-receptor mutation. Where the block is high in the pathway the adrenal steroids are affected too and there is accompanying adrenal insufficiency.
Presentation
Undervirilization of a 46,XY infant, along a spectrum from mild hypospadias to entirely female-appearing external genitalia. There is no uterus, because AMH is unaffected. Wolffian development is variable and follows how much testosterone was actually made.
The useful test, and the treatment
These infants respond to testosterone therapy, and that response is diagnostically informative — it separates a synthesis defect from androgen insensitivity, where exogenous testosterone does nothing. Precursor-to-product ratios locate the specific block.
Pathophysiology
Autosomal recessive loss of 5α-reductase type 2, so testosterone is not converted to DHT in the genital skin. Testosterone maintains the Wolffian ducts perfectly well; DHT is what virilizes the external genitalia, and it is missing.
Presentation at birth
Minimal external male development: an enlarged clitorophallus, an incomplete scrotum, undescended testes, a perineal urethral opening — while internal "male" ducts are present and there is no uterus. Many are assigned female at birth.
Presentation at puberty
The large pubertal rise in testosterone acts directly on the androgen receptor and produces virilization: increased muscle mass, increased phallic size, deepening voice, onset of erections. This is the phenomenon described in the Dominican Republic cohorts as "guevedoce", where the local prevalence has been reported as high as 1 in 90 male births in an affected community.
Evaluation
A raised testosterone-to-DHT ratio, particularly after hCG stimulation, plus SRD5A2 genotyping. The prospect of virilization at puberty is a central consideration in sex-of-rearing discussions, and one of the strongest arguments for not performing irreversible surgery in infancy.
Pathophysiology
An X-linked defect of the androgen receptor. Androgen biosynthesis is entirely normal — the hormone is there and the tissue cannot hear it. AMH is unaffected, so the Müllerian ducts still regress: no uterus, no tubes, no upper vagina. The Wolffian ducts, which need androgen, do not develop.
CAIS
Phenotypically female external genitalia with a short blind-ending vaginal pouch and no uterus. Testes are intra-abdominal or inguinal — sometimes presenting as an inguinal hernia in an apparently female infant. Little or no axillary and pubic hair, because that hair is androgen-dependent. Testosterone is normal for the male reference range. Breast development at puberty is normal, because testosterone aromatizes to estrogen and there is no androgen opposition. The usual presentation is primary amenorrhea in an otherwise typically developing adolescent.
PAIS
A spectrum, from a phenotypic female with mild virilization through frankly ambiguous genitalia to an undervirilized male with hypospadias, micropenis, or gynecomastia. Management is correspondingly individualized and considerably harder than in CAIS.
Long-term care
Estrogen replacement after gonadectomy, vaginal management where needed, psychological support, and a discussion about gonadal malignancy risk and the timing of gonadectomy — the modern approach is to defer, since the risk in CAIS is low before puberty and the gonads provide the estrogen that drives normal pubertal development.

Sex chromosome DSD

Objective 5. Here the first step — chromosomal sex — is itself atypical, so the gonad is the structure that suffers.

Definition
Both ovarian and testicular tissue in the same individual. The possible arrangements are two ovotestes; one ovotestis with an ovary or a testis; one ovary and one testis; or one ovotestis and one streak gonad.
Presentation
Highly variable, because the phenotype depends on how much functioning testicular tissue there is and where it sits. Both Müllerian and Wolffian structures or remnants are typically present, often asymmetrically — a Müllerian structure on the ovarian side where no local AMH was produced.
Karyotype and frequency
A variety of chromosomal patterns; the commonest in most reported series is 46,XX, with 46,XX/46,XY chimerism and 46,XY also described. It is uncommon — under 10% of DSD in the United States.
Mixed gonadal dysgenesis
A streak gonad on one side and a testis (or ovary) on the other. A streak is an underdeveloped, non-functional gonad replaced by fibrous connective tissue. The karyotype is usually mosaic — 45,X/46,XY, sometimes 46,XX/46,XY. The phenotype varies widely and is characteristically asymmetric, which is the physical clue. Because Y-derived material sits in a dysgenetic gonad, the risk of gonadoblastoma is substantial — reported at roughly 15–25% — and gonadal management and surveillance are part of the plan.
Complete (pure) gonadal dysgenesis
Bilateral streak gonads, usually with a 46,XX karyotype, sometimes 45,X — and the 46,XY form is Swyer syndrome. Because there is no AMH and no testosterone, the phenotype is female, internally and externally, including a uterus. The presentation is therefore delayed or absent puberty with gonadal failure and high gonadotropins, not genital ambiguity. Malignancy risk is not increased unless Y chromosome material is present — and where it is, the risk again approaches 25%, which is what drives the recommendation for gonadectomy in that group.
Genetics
Complete or partial absence of the second sex chromosome; 45,X is the commonest single karyotype, with mosaics and structural X abnormalities making up much of the rest. Loss of one copy of SHOX accounts for the short stature. Reported in roughly 1 in 2,000 to 2,500 live female births.
The two near-universal features
Short stature and ovarian failure. The gonads are streaks, so estrogen is absent and LH and FSH are high — hypergonadotropic hypogonadism. Expect delayed or absent puberty, premature ovarian insufficiency, and infertility.
The rest of the phenotype
Neonatal lymphedema of the hands and feet, webbed neck, low posterior hairline, left-sided cardiac anomalies — bicuspid aortic valve and coarctation, with aortic dissection the feared complication — renal anomalies, and orthopedic anomalies. Also hearing loss, hypothyroidism, and specific learning differences.
The presentation to recognize
A short girl with delayed puberty, often with a cardiac anomaly already known. Any girl with unexplained short stature or primary amenorrhea deserves a karyotype.
Genetics
One or more extra X chromosomes — 47,XXY most often, with 48,XXXY and higher-grade variants producing more severe phenotypes. Among the commonest chromosomal conditions, generally quoted at around 1 in 500 to 1,000 male births, and substantially under-diagnosed.
The endocrine lesion
Primary testicular failure. The seminiferous tubules degenerate and are replaced by hyaline, giving small, firm testes and azoospermia. Leydig cell function is also impaired, so testosterone is low while LH and FSH are high — hypergonadotropic hypogonadism, the same pattern as Turner syndrome and for the same reason.
The phenotype that follows
Low androgen means poor muscle development, a more typically female fat distribution, gynecomastia, and sparse facial hair — although pubic and axillary hair are often normal, since adrenal androgen covers that. Tall stature, attributed to the extra SHOX copy and to delayed epiphyseal closure. Eunuchoid proportions. Learning and language differences are common.
The presentation to recognize
A tall adolescent male with delayed or stalled puberty and small testes, with high LH and FSH and low testosterone. Management is testosterone replacement and fertility counselling — sperm retrieval is sometimes possible.
Mechanism
GnRH neurons fail to migrate from the olfactory placode to the hypothalamus during development. Because those neurons travel with the olfactory axons, the same failure costs both. Genes include ANOS1/KAL1 (X-linked), FGFR1, CHD7, PROK2, and PROKR2.
Presentation
Hypogonadotropic hypogonadism plus anosmia. Delayed puberty with absent testicular development, or primary amenorrhea. Micropenis and cryptorchidism may be present at birth.
Why it sits at the end of this list
It is the opposite biochemical pattern to Turner and Klinefelter: low LH and FSH with low sex steroids — the signal is missing, not the gonad. The karyotype is normal, which is why it is not truly a DSD. Diagnosis: low gonadotropins with low testosterone or estradiol, MRI showing hypoplastic or absent olfactory bulbs, and genetic testing. Treatment is sex steroid replacement, with gonadotropin therapy when fertility is the goal.

Newborn screening for congenital adrenal hyperplasia

Objective 6, and the clearest example on this page of a screening programme designed around one specific failure mode.

How it detects classic CAH
A heel-stick dried blood spot is assayed for 17-hydroxyprogesterone, the precursor immediately above the 21-hydroxylase block. In classic CAH it is markedly elevated — values in the thousands of ng/dL against a normal of tens. A positive screen triggers same-day confirmatory serum testing and electrolytes.
Why salt-wasting CAH needs urgent diagnosis
Because the crisis arrives at 10 to 21 days of life in an infant who was well at birth and discharged home. Untreated it is hyponatremia, hyperkalemia, hypoglycemia, hypovolemic shock, and death. There is no window for a leisurely workup: the screen has to find these infants before the crisis, not during it.
Why male infants are the target
A 46,XX infant with classic CAH is virilized at birth, so the genital findings themselves prompt evaluation. A 46,XY infant looks completely normal — there is nothing to see, no reason to test, and the first sign is the crisis. Newborn screening exists primarily to catch that infant. Its second benefit is preventing incorrect sex assignment of a severely virilized 46,XX infant.
The cost of the design, stated honestly

The cut-off is set low, deliberately, to maximize sensitivity — missing a case means a dead infant, so the programme accepts a great many false positives to avoid that. The consequence is poor specificity and a high false-positive rate, and the false positives are concentrated in preterm, sick, and physiologically stressed infants, in whom 17-OHP is elevated for entirely benign reasons. That is why gestational-age-adjusted cut-offs are used, why a second-tier assay is increasingly added, and why a positive screen is a reason to test, not a diagnosis.

Sex assignment and long-term management

Objective 7. This is where the field has changed most, and the exam question is usually about why it changed.

The traditional paradigm, and what it got right
Developed in the 1950s by John Money and Lawson Wilkins. It was deeply critical of assigning sex on any single criterion — not on chromosomes alone, not on gonads alone — and it insisted on a thorough workup: identify the etiology, assess what interventions are possible, and choose the option most congruent with anticipated development at puberty and in adulthood. Those parts have survived. Where it went wrong was the final step: early "normalizing" genital surgery, performed to remove parental doubt and, it was believed, to enable gender identity to develop properly.
Why that final step was abandoned
The claim was empirical, and it did not hold up. There is no evidence that early feminizing genital surgery improves psychosocial outcome, and there is increasing evidence of harm — from small studies, long-term follow-up, and patient testimony: loss of sexual function and sensation, repeated operations, and distress where the assigned sex did not match the person's eventual gender identity. The asymmetry that settles the argument is stated on the slide in seven words: sex assignment is reversible, surgery is not.
The questions the team asks now

Who is the patient? The infant — not the parents, however genuine their distress. Is this treatment medically necessary? Distinguish interventions needed for health (relieving urinary obstruction, treating adrenal insufficiency, managing malignancy risk) from those performed for appearance. Can it wait? If it can be deferred safely, defer it. Until when? Until gender identity is established and the person can participate in the decision, keeping in mind that gender identity may not match the sex assigned in infancy.

The patient-centered paradigm, in six commitments

Treat the medical issues — adrenal insufficiency, hypoglycemia, obstruction, malignancy risk — promptly and fully. Do not forcibly normalize. Avoid stigmatizing language, examinations, and photography. Elective surgical and hormonal interventions should be chosen by the patient when they are able to choose. Address psychosocial distress directly, with mental health professionals as core team members rather than an afterthought. Always tell the truth — to the family throughout, and to the patient in an age-appropriate way as they grow. Concealment was standard practice for decades and is one of the harms patients report most consistently.

What still has to be decided early

Deferring surgery is not the same as deferring everything. A sex of rearing is still assigned, after full evaluation and in partnership with the family, because a child needs one to live in the world — and it is chosen using diagnosis, expected pubertal course, fertility potential, and the outcome data for that specific condition. Some decisions genuinely cannot wait: glucocorticoid and mineralocorticoid replacement in CAH, gonadal malignancy surveillance where Y material is present in a dysgenetic gonad, and relief of urinary obstruction. The principle is not "do nothing" — it is do what is necessary now, and leave what is elective to the person it belongs to.

Retrieval prompts

Complete androgen insensitivity and 46,XY complete gonadal dysgenesis (Swyer syndrome). The uterus is the discriminator, and it tells you whether a functioning testis ever existed. In CAIS a testis formed and made AMH, so the Müllerian ducts regressed — no uterus — while the androgen receptor defect prevented any androgen effect. In complete gonadal dysgenesis no testis formed at all, so there was neither AMH nor testosterone: the Müllerian ducts persisted into a uterus and tubes, and the gonads are streaks. Testosterone separates them too — normal male range in CAIS, undetectable in dysgenesis.

Because the two tissues need different hormones. The Wolffian ducts are maintained by testosterone itself, which is made normally. The external genitalia require conversion to dihydrotestosterone in the target tissue, which is the step that is missing. So the epididymis, vas, and seminal vesicles are present while the external genitalia are not virilized — and there is still no uterus, because AMH was never affected. At puberty the sheer size of the testosterone surge produces partial virilization by acting directly on the receptor.

Congenital adrenal hyperplasia in a severely virilized 46,XX infant. Ovaries do not descend, so bilaterally non-palpable gonads in an apparently male infant should raise the possibility that this is not a male infant at all. Two things follow: the sex of rearing may be wrong, and — far more urgently — that infant is on a trajectory toward a salt-wasting adrenal crisis at 10 to 21 days. Send a karyotype and a 17-hydroxyprogesterone, and follow electrolytes and glucose over the first two weeks rather than checking them once.

High LH/FSH with low sex steroids — hypergonadotropic hypogonadism: the gonad has failed and feedback is lost. Turner syndrome, Klinefelter syndrome, gonadal dysgenesis. Low LH/FSH with low sex steroids — hypogonadotropic hypogonadism: the signal is missing, and the gonad was never asked. Kallmann syndrome, hypopituitarism, functional causes. Normal-to-high LH with normal male testosterone in a phenotypic female: the hormone is present and the tissue cannot respond — androgen insensitivity. Reading the trophic hormone against the target hormone works here exactly as it does in the thyroid and adrenal axes.

Because the two errors are not equivalent. A false positive costs a repeat blood test and a few days of family anxiety. A false negative costs an infant who was discharged well and dies of an unrecognized salt-wasting crisis in the third week of life — one that is entirely preventable with hydrocortisone, fludrocortisone, saline, and dextrose. When the cost of missing a case is that asymmetric, you set the cut-off for sensitivity and accept the specificity you get. It also explains why preterm and sick infants generate most of the false positives, and why gestational-age-adjusted cut-offs and second-tier assays are used to claw specificity back without giving up sensitivity.
