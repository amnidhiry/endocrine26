DSD Concepts

Sexual development happens in three sequential steps — chromosomal, then gonadal, then phenotypic — and a difference of sex development is a break at one of them. Learn the normal sequence properly and the diagnoses sort themselves: the karyotype tells you which step to interrogate, the gonads tell you whether the second step worked, and the external genitalia tell you what hormones the fetus actually saw. One of these diagnoses can kill an infant in the second week of life, so the evaluation is urgent as well as careful.

Three steps, in order

Original diagram. Read the three bands as three independent questions, because that is how the diseases behave. Chromosomal: what karyotype? Gonadal: did a testis form? Phenotypic: were AMH, testosterone, and DHT each delivered and each received? Almost every diagnosis on this page is one of those signals failing while the others work — which is why the phenotypes are so specific.

Chromosomal sex
Set at conception. The determining element is SRY — the sex-determining region on the short arm of the Y chromosome — a switch that turns on the Sertoli cell programme. SRY is the trigger, not the whole mechanism.
Gonadal sex
The bipotential gonad differentiates at around six weeks post-conception. With SRY it becomes a testis; without SRY, an ovary. Everything downstream depends on which formed, because the phenotype is driven by the hormones the gonad makes.
Phenotypic sex
Driven by the gonad's output. Internal ducts: both Müllerian and Wolffian systems are present initially and one set regresses. External genitalia: a single bipotential anlage that virilizes if it sees enough DHT.
The three hormonal signals, and what each one does

AMH, from Sertoli cells — regresses the Müllerian ducts (uterus, tubes, upper vagina). Acts locally, so a unilateral testis regresses only its own side. Testosterone, from Leydig cells — maintains the Wolffian ducts (epididymis, vas deferens, seminal vesicles). Also local and high-concentration, which is why circulating adrenal androgen cannot substitute for it. DHT, made from testosterone by 5α-reductase in the target tissue — the virilizer of the external genitalia. Testosterone alone will not do this job, and that single fact explains an entire disease.

Timing decides what the virilization looks like

In a 46,XX fetus, androgen before ~13 weeks — while the labioscrotal folds can still fuse — gives posterior labial fusion and a urogenital sinus. Androgen from ~14 weeks onward, once those structures have formed, gives clitoromegaly without fusion. So the type of virilization tells you when the androgen arrived.

Why a DSD is both a medical and a psychosocial emergency

Objective 1. There are two independent reasons, and they need different responses.

The medical emergency
DSDs occur in roughly 1 in 5,000 live births, and some of these infants are in the first days of a life-threatening endocrine illness. Congenital adrenal hyperplasia — the commonest cause of a 46,XX DSD — produces adrenal crisis at 10 to 21 days of life: vomiting, poor feeding, failure to thrive, dehydration, hyponatremia, hyperkalemia, hypoglycemia, shock. The infant looks well at birth. Panhypopituitarism is the other one to fear, with hypoglycemia and micropenis. Both are treatable and both are lethal untreated — which is what makes the evaluation urgent rather than merely thorough.
The psychosocial emergency
"Is it a boy or a girl?" is the first question anyone asks about a newborn, and the family cannot answer it. The consequences: acute parental distress, disrupted bonding, pressure from relatives, and irreversible decisions made under time pressure. The response is not speed for its own sake — it is rapid, coordinated evaluation with honest ongoing communication.
How the team communicates while the workup runs

Neutral anatomical language — clitorophallus, labioscrotal folds, gonads — and no pronouns until sex of rearing is agreed. Do not guess at a provisional answer that may have to be retracted. Involve the parents from the beginning rather than presenting them with conclusions. And the team must speak with one voice: contradictory statements from different specialists are among the most damaging things that can happen to a family here.

The findings that warrant evaluation

Objective 2. Note that several of these occur in an infant whose genitalia look unremarkable at a glance.

External genital findings
  • Apparent female — clitoromegaly, posterior labial fusion, a single perineal opening (urogenital sinus), or an inguinal or labial mass, which may be a testis.
  • Apparent male — bilateral non-palpable gonads, severe hypospadias (particularly perineal or penoscrotal), hypospadias with undescended testis, micropenis, or a bifid scrotum.
  • Either direction — frankly ambiguous genitalia.
Findings away from the genitalia
  • Signs of adrenal insufficiency — poor feeding, vomiting, failure to thrive, dehydration, hypotension, hyperpigmentation of the genital skin and areolae, hypoglycemia, hyponatremia with hyperkalemia.
  • Associated congenital anomalies, since several DSDs are syndromic.
  • Discordance between prenatal karyotype and appearance at birth.
  • A family history of DSD, consanguinity, unexplained neonatal death, or infertility.
One physical sign carries more weight than any other

A palpable gonad is almost always a testis, because ovaries do not descend. So a palpable gonad makes a 46,XX DSD unlikely and reorients the workup toward an undervirilized 46,XY infant, ovotesticular DSD, or gonadal dysgenesis. Symmetry matters too: symmetric findings suggest a hormonal cause, asymmetric findings suggest dysgenesis. And bilaterally non-palpable gonads in an apparently male infant are CAH until excluded — that infant may be a virilized 46,XX girl heading for a salt-wasting crisis.

The workup

Examination, documented precisely
  • Phallic length and diameter.
  • Labioscrotal folds — fused or not, and how far posteriorly.
  • Position of the urethral meatus.
  • Anogenital ratio.
  • Palpable gonads — present or absent, and which side.
  • Hyperpigmentation, pointing to high ACTH and so to CAH.

Plus a general examination for dysmorphic features.

First-line laboratory studies
  • Karyotype — with FISH for SRY where relevant — as the first branch point.
  • 17-hydroxyprogesterone, because CAH is the diagnosis that cannot wait.
  • Serum electrolytes and glucose, followed serially through the first two weeks.
  • Testosterone, DHT, AMH, LH and FSH, and further steroid precursors as the pattern directs.
Imaging
  • Pelvic and abdominal ultrasound first: is there a uterus, are there intra-abdominal or streak gonads, is there adrenal hyperplasia.
  • Genitogram to define a urogenital sinus and the anatomy of the vagina and urethra.
  • MRI or CT of the abdomen and pelvis when the ultrasound leaves questions open.
Who is on the team

Assembled at once rather than sequentially: neonatology or pediatrics, endocrinology, urology, pediatric surgery, gynecology, genetics, and mental health. The operational rule is the one already stated — speak with one voice.

46,XX DSD — the over-virilized

Objective 3. Ovaries, a uterus, and a fetus that saw androgen it should not have seen.

Pathophysiology
Autosomal recessive; an inborn error of cortisol and aldosterone production, over 90% of cases from 21-hydroxylase deficiency. Low cortisol removes feedback, ACTH rises, the adrenals become hyperplastic, and the accumulated precursor is shunted into the androgen branch. The full derivation is on the steroid synthesis page.
Presentation
  • A 46,XX infant with atypical genitalia — clitoromegaly, labioscrotal fusion, a urogenital sinus — and no palpable gonads, because the ovaries are intra-abdominal.
  • Internal structures (uterus, tubes, ovaries) are normal.
  • Then, in the salt-wasting form, adrenal crisis at 10–21 days: failure to thrive, progressive weight loss, dehydration, high potassium, low sodium, low cortisol, shock.

Hyperpigmentation of the genital skin is a useful early clue.

Evaluation
  • 17-hydroxyprogesterone — markedly raised.
  • Karyotype 46,XX.
  • Ultrasound — a uterus, and often hyperplastic adrenals.
  • Electrolytes and glucose, repeated: the crisis develops over days, and a normal sodium on day 2 means nothing on day 12.
Why it is first on every list
It is both the commonest cause of a 46,XX DSD and the only cause of atypical genitalia that is immediately life-threatening. Every newborn genital-anomaly workup is built around excluding it first. Classic CAH is quoted at roughly 1 in 5,000 to 10,000 births.
Pathophysiology
Aromatase normally converts fetal androgens to estrogens in the placenta, protecting both fetus and pregnant person. Without it — CYP19A1, autosomal recessive — those androgens cross unchanged.
Presentation

Both the baby and the pregnant person virilize.

  • The pregnant person virilizes too — acne, hirsutism, and voice change during the pregnancy, which regress after delivery.
  • The 46,XX infant has atypical genitalia, from clitoromegaly to complete labioscrotal fusion.
  • Later — absent puberty with tall stature, ovarian cysts, and low bone density from estrogen deficiency.
How it is distinguished from CAH
Maternal virilization is the discriminator — in CAH the mother is unaffected. Biochemically, 17-OHP is normal, with low estrogens and high androgens. A maternal luteoma or exogenous androgen gives a similar fetal picture and is excluded by history.
Pathophysiology
During paternal meiosis, SRY translocates onto an X chromosome. The 46,XX embryo receives the testis-determining signal, so the bipotential gonad becomes a testis and the phenotype develops along male lines.
Presentation
Usually a phenotypic male, sometimes with hypospadias or small testes, often not identified until an adult infertility work-up. Because the rest of the Y — including the azoospermia factor region — is absent, there is azoospermia, and testosterone often falls short at puberty with a compensatory rise in gonadotropins.
Evaluation
Karyotype 46,XX with a FISH probe positive for SRY (or chromosomal microarray). Long-term care is testosterone replacement if needed, and fertility counselling.

46,XY DSD — the under-virilized

Objective 4. A testis formed. Something after that failed — production, conversion, or reception.

Sort them by which of the three signals failed

AMH failed → Müllerian structures persist in an otherwise male infant. Testosterone production failed → both duct systems and the external genitalia are underdeveloped. Conversion to DHT failed → internal male structures are present, external genitalia are not virilized. Reception failed → nothing androgen-dependent forms at all, but AMH still worked, so there is no uterus. That last contrast — no androgen effect with no Müllerian structures — is the fingerprint of androgen insensitivity.

Pathophysiology
Absent AMH or a defective AMH receptor. Sertoli cells fail to deliver the regression signal, so the Müllerian ducts persist. Leydig cell function is normal, so testosterone is made and the Wolffian structures are also present — the one condition where both duct systems coexist.
Presentation
Phenotypically male external genitalia with undescended testes, tethered by the Müllerian remnants. Inguinal hernia is common, and the diagnosis is classically made at hernia repair or orchiopexy when a uterus and tubes are found unexpectedly.
Management
Orchiopexy, with careful handling of the Müllerian remnants and the vas, which run close together. Long-term concerns are fertility and the malignancy risk of cryptorchidism.
Pathophysiology
A defect can sit at almost any step from cholesterol to testosterone — StAR, CYP11A1, 3β-HSD, CYP17A1, 17β-HSD — or in the Leydig cell itself, through hypoplasia or an inactivating LH-receptor mutation. A block high in the pathway affects adrenal steroids too, adding adrenal insufficiency.
Presentation
Undervirilization of a 46,XY infant, from mild hypospadias to entirely female-appearing external genitalia. There is no uterus, because AMH is unaffected. Wolffian development follows how much testosterone was actually made.
The useful test, and the treatment
These infants respond to testosterone therapy, and that response is itself diagnostic — it separates a synthesis defect from androgen insensitivity, where exogenous testosterone does nothing. Precursor-to-product ratios locate the specific block.
Pathophysiology
Autosomal recessive loss of 5α-reductase type 2, so testosterone is not converted to DHT in the genital skin. Testosterone maintains the Wolffian ducts perfectly well; DHT is what virilizes the external genitalia, and it is missing.
Presentation at birth
Minimal external male development: an enlarged clitorophallus, an incomplete scrotum, undescended testes, a perineal urethral opening — while internal "male" ducts are present and there is no uterus. Many are assigned female at birth.
Presentation at puberty
The large pubertal testosterone rise acts directly on the androgen receptor and produces virilization: increased muscle mass, increased phallic size, onset of erections. So a child assigned female at birth virilizes at puberty — the phenomenon the lecture calls "guevedoce", after a Dominican Republic cohort in which the local rate reached 1 in 90 male births.
Evaluation
A raised testosterone-to-DHT ratio, particularly after hCG stimulation, plus SRD5A2 genotyping. The prospect of virilization at puberty is a central consideration in sex-of-rearing discussions, and one of the strongest arguments for not performing irreversible surgery in infancy.
Pathophysiology
An X-linked defect of the androgen receptor. Androgen biosynthesis is entirely normal — the hormone is there and the tissue cannot hear it. AMH is unaffected, so the Müllerian ducts still regress: no uterus, no tubes, no upper vagina. The Wolffian ducts, which need androgen, do not develop.
CAIS
  • Phenotypically female external genitalia with a short blind-ending vaginal pouch and no uterus.
  • Testes are intra-abdominal or inguinal, sometimes presenting as an inguinal hernia in an apparently female infant.
  • Little or no axillary and pubic hair, which is androgen-dependent.
  • Testosterone is normal for the male range, and breast development at puberty is normal, because testosterone aromatizes to estrogen with no androgen opposition.

The usual presentation is primary amenorrhea.

PAIS
A spectrum — phenotypic female with mild virilization, frankly ambiguous genitalia, or an undervirilized male with hypospadias, micropenis, or gynecomastia. Management is individualized and harder than in CAIS.
Long-term care
  • Estrogen replacement after gonadectomy.
  • Vaginal management where needed, and psychological support.
  • A discussion of gonadal malignancy risk and the timing of gonadectomy — the modern approach is to defer, since the risk in CAIS is low before puberty and the gonads supply the estrogen that drives pubertal development.

Sex chromosome DSD

Objective 5. Here the first step — chromosomal sex — is itself atypical, so the gonad is the structure that suffers.

Definition

Both ovarian and testicular tissue in the same individual. The possible arrangements are:

  • two ovotestes;
  • one ovotestis with an ovary or a testis;
  • one ovary and one testis;
  • one ovotestis and one streak gonad.
Presentation
Highly variable, depending on how much functioning testicular tissue there is and where it sits. Both Müllerian and Wolffian structures or remnants are typically present, often asymmetrically — a Müllerian structure on the ovarian side, where no local AMH was produced.
Karyotype
A variety of patterns; the commonest is 46,XX, with 46,XX/46,XY chimerism and 46,XY also described. Uncommon — under 10% of DSD in the United States.
Mixed gonadal dysgenesis
A streak gonad on one side and a testis (or ovary) on the other. A streak is an underdeveloped, non-functional gonad replaced by fibrous tissue. The karyotype is usually mosaic — 45,X/46,XY. The phenotype varies widely and is characteristically asymmetric, which is the physical clue. Because Y-derived material sits in a dysgenetic gonad, the risk of gonadoblastoma is substantial (~15–25%), so gonadal management and surveillance are part of the plan.
Complete (pure) gonadal dysgenesis
Bilateral streak gonads, usually 46,XX; the 46,XY form is Swyer syndrome. With no AMH and no testosterone, the phenotype is female internally and externally, uterus included. So the presentation is delayed or absent puberty with high gonadotropins, not genital ambiguity. Malignancy risk is not increased unless Y material is present — where it is, the risk again approaches 25% and gonadectomy is recommended.
Genetics
Complete or partial absence of the second sex chromosome; 45,X is the commonest single karyotype, with mosaics and structural X abnormalities making up much of the rest. Loss of one copy of SHOX accounts for the short stature. Roughly 1 in 2,000 live births.
The two near-universal features
Short stature and ovarian failure. The gonads are streaks, so estrogen is absent and LH and FSH are high — hypergonadotropic hypogonadism, with delayed or absent puberty and infertility.
The rest of the phenotype
  • Neonatal lymphedema of the hands and feet.
  • Webbed neck and a low posterior hairline.
  • Left-sided cardiac anomalies — bicuspid aortic valve and coarctation, with aortic dissection the feared complication.
  • Renal and orthopedic anomalies.
The presentation to recognize
A short girl with delayed puberty, often with a cardiac anomaly already known. Any girl with unexplained short stature or primary amenorrhea deserves a karyotype.
Genetics
One or more extra X chromosomes — 47,XXY most often, with higher-grade variants giving more severe phenotypes. Among the commonest chromosomal conditions, around 1 in 1,000 live births, and substantially under-diagnosed.
The endocrine lesion
Primary testicular failure. The seminiferous tubules degenerate and are replaced by hyaline, giving small, firm testes and azoospermia. Leydig function is also impaired, so testosterone is low while LH and FSH are high — hypergonadotropic hypogonadism, the same pattern as Turner and for the same reason.
The phenotype that follows

Low androgen gives:

  • Poor muscle development and a more typically female fat distribution.
  • Gynecomastia.
  • Sparse facial hair — though pubic and axillary hair are often normal, since adrenal androgen covers that.
  • Tall stature with eunuchoid proportions, from the extra SHOX copy and delayed epiphyseal closure.
The presentation to recognize
A tall adolescent male with delayed or stalled puberty and small testes, with high LH and FSH and low testosterone. Manage with testosterone replacement and fertility counselling.
Mechanism
GnRH neurons fail to migrate from the olfactory placode to the hypothalamus. Because those neurons travel with the olfactory axons, the same failure costs both. Classically KAL1/ANOS1 mutations.
Presentation
Hypogonadotropic hypogonadism plus anosmia. Delayed puberty with absent testicular development, or primary amenorrhea. Micropenis and cryptorchidism may be present at birth.
Why it sits at the end of this list
It is the opposite biochemical pattern to Turner and Klinefelter: low LH and FSH with low sex steroids — the signal is missing, not the gonad. The karyotype is normal, which is why it is not truly a DSD. Diagnosis: low gonadotropins with low testosterone or estradiol, plus MRI showing hypoplastic or absent olfactory bulbs. Treat with sex steroid replacement, or gonadotropins when fertility is the goal.

Newborn screening for congenital adrenal hyperplasia

Objective 6, and the clearest example on this page of a screening programme designed around one specific failure mode.

How it detects classic CAH
A heel-stick dried blood spot is assayed for 17-hydroxyprogesterone, the precursor immediately above the 21-hydroxylase block. In classic CAH it is markedly elevated. A positive screen triggers same-day confirmatory serum testing and electrolytes.
Why salt-wasting CAH needs urgent diagnosis
The crisis arrives at 10 to 21 days of life in an infant who was well at birth and discharged home: hyponatremia, hyperkalemia, hypoglycemia, shock, death. The screen has to find these infants before the crisis, not during it.
Why male infants are the target
A 46,XX infant with classic CAH is virilized at birth, so the genital findings prompt evaluation. A 46,XY infant looks completely normal — nothing to see, no reason to test, and the first sign is the crisis. Screening exists primarily to catch that infant; its second benefit is preventing incorrect sex assignment of a severely virilized 46,XX infant.
The cost of the design

The cut-off is set low on purpose, to maximize sensitivity — missing a case means a dead infant. The price is specificity: the lecture quotes it at 2%, a 98% false-positive rate, concentrated in preterm, sick, and stressed infants, in whom 17-OHP is raised for benign reasons. Hence the rule that a positive screen is a reason to test, not a diagnosis.

The algorithm underneath those cases

Step 1 — karyotype. It splits the differential into the three columns of the classification table. Step 2 — palpable gonads, and symmetry. Palpable means testicular tissue; asymmetry means dysgenesis. Step 3 — 17-hydroxyprogesterone and electrolytes. This is the step that finds the infant who is about to become critically unwell, and it happens in parallel with the other two rather than after them. Step 4 — is there a uterus on ultrasound? A uterus means AMH was never delivered; no uterus means it was. Step 5 — the hormone axis. Testosterone, DHT and their ratio, AMH, LH and FSH: high gonadotropins with low sex steroids localizes to the gonad, low gonadotropins with low sex steroids localizes above it.

ActSex assignment and long-term management

Sex assignment and long-term management

Objective 7. This is where the field has changed most, and the exam question is usually about why it changed.

The traditional paradigm, and what it got right
Developed in the 1950s by Money and Wilkins. It was rightly critical of assigning sex on any single criterion — not chromosomes alone, not gonads alone — and insisted on a thorough workup: identify the etiology, then choose the option most congruent with expected development at puberty and in adulthood. Those parts survived. Where it went wrong was the final step: early "normalizing" genital surgery, done to remove parental doubt and, it was believed, to let gender identity develop properly.
Why that final step was abandoned
The claim was empirical and it did not hold up. There is no evidence that early feminizing genital surgery improves psychosocial outcome, and there is increasing evidence of harm: loss of sexual function and sensation, repeated operations, and distress where the assigned sex did not match the person's eventual gender identity. The asymmetry that settles it: sex assignment is reversible, surgery is not.
The current approach

Four questions frame every decision. Who is the patient? The infant — not the parents, however genuine their distress. Is this medically necessary? Separate what health requires (adrenal insufficiency, urinary obstruction, malignancy risk) from what is done for appearance. Can it wait? If it can be deferred safely, defer it. Until when? Until gender identity is established and the person can take part in the decision. The commitments that follow: treat the medical issues promptly, do not forcibly normalize, avoid stigmatizing language, examinations, and photography, leave elective surgery and hormones to the patient's own choice, address psychosocial distress with mental health as a core team member, and always tell the truth — concealment was standard for decades and is one of the harms patients report most.

What still has to be decided early

Deferring surgery is not deferring everything. A sex of rearing is still assigned after full evaluation and in partnership with the family, chosen from the diagnosis, expected pubertal course, fertility potential, and outcome data for that specific condition. And some things genuinely cannot wait: glucocorticoid and mineralocorticoid replacement in CAH, malignancy surveillance where Y material sits in a dysgenetic gonad, and relief of urinary obstruction. The principle is not "do nothing" — it is do what is necessary now, and leave what is elective to the person it belongs to.

Retrieval prompts

Complete androgen insensitivity and 46,XY complete gonadal dysgenesis (Swyer syndrome). The uterus is the discriminator, and it tells you whether a functioning testis ever existed. In CAIS a testis formed and made AMH, so the Müllerian ducts regressed — no uterus — while the androgen receptor defect prevented any androgen effect. In complete gonadal dysgenesis no testis formed at all, so there was neither AMH nor testosterone: the Müllerian ducts persisted into a uterus and tubes, and the gonads are streaks. Testosterone separates them too — normal male range in CAIS, undetectable in dysgenesis.

Because the two tissues need different hormones. The Wolffian ducts are maintained by testosterone itself, which is made normally. The external genitalia require conversion to dihydrotestosterone in the target tissue, which is the step that is missing. So the epididymis, vas, and seminal vesicles are present while the external genitalia are not virilized — and there is still no uterus, because AMH was never affected. At puberty the sheer size of the testosterone surge produces partial virilization by acting directly on the receptor.

Congenital adrenal hyperplasia in a severely virilized 46,XX infant. Ovaries do not descend, so bilaterally non-palpable gonads in an apparently male infant should raise the possibility that this is not a male infant at all. Two things follow: the sex of rearing may be wrong, and — far more urgently — that infant is on a trajectory toward a salt-wasting adrenal crisis at 10 to 21 days. Send a karyotype and a 17-hydroxyprogesterone, and follow electrolytes and glucose over the first two weeks rather than checking them once.

High LH/FSH with low sex steroids — hypergonadotropic hypogonadism: the gonad has failed and feedback is lost. Turner syndrome, Klinefelter syndrome, gonadal dysgenesis. Low LH/FSH with low sex steroids — hypogonadotropic hypogonadism: the signal is missing, and the gonad was never asked. Kallmann syndrome, hypopituitarism, functional causes. Normal-to-high LH with normal male testosterone in a phenotypic female: the hormone is present and the tissue cannot respond — androgen insensitivity. Reading the trophic hormone against the target hormone works here exactly as it does in the thyroid and adrenal axes.

Because the two errors are not equivalent. A false positive costs a repeat blood test and a few days of family anxiety. A false negative costs an infant who was discharged well and dies of an unrecognized salt-wasting crisis in the third week of life — one that is entirely preventable with hydrocortisone, fludrocortisone, saline, and dextrose. When the cost of missing a case is that asymmetric, you set the cut-off for sensitivity and accept the specificity you get — here about 2%. It also explains why preterm, sick, and stressed infants generate most of the false positives.
