Adrenal Gland Disorders

Four hormones come out of this gland — aldosterone, cortisol, adrenal androgens, and catecholamines — and each one can be present in excess or absent. That gives you eight boxes, and nearly every disorder in this session lives in one of them. Work out which hormone is wrong and in which direction, then ask whether the fault is in the gland or in the signal driving it. Everything else is detail.

Orientation: four hormones, two control systems

The three stated objectives, and how this page delivers them

1. Associate symptoms, signs, and diagnostic findings with the underlying disorder — the aldosterone, cortisol excess, insufficiency, CAH, neoplasm, pheochromocytoma, and MEN sections, each written presentation-first. 2. Prescribe the correct first-line diagnostic test — every disorder names its own first test, and one table collects all of them so you can rehearse the choice in isolation. 3. Discuss management of primary, secondary, and tertiary causes based on case characteristics and test results — the management section, plus adrenal crisis, which is the one you must be able to do without looking anything up.

Cortex — three zones
Glomerulosa → aldosterone, answering to angiotensin II and potassium. Fasciculata → cortisol, answering to ACTH. Reticularis → DHEA and androstenedione, also ACTH-responsive. The synthetic pathway itself is worked through on the steroid synthesis page.
Medulla — a different organ
Neural-crest-derived chromaffin cells making catecholamines. Not part of the steroid story at all, not under ACTH control, and the source of pheochromocytoma. It is inside the same capsule; that is the whole relationship.
The two questions to ask every time
Which hormone, and in which direction? That names the syndrome. Gland or signal? That names the level — primary means the gland, secondary means the pituitary, tertiary means the hypothalamus, and for aldosterone "secondary" means the renin–angiotensin system is driving it.

Original diagram. The identical logic applied to aldosterone and renin gives you the hyperaldosteronism split: aldosterone high with renin suppressed is primary, both high is secondary. One rule, two axes, most of this lecture.

RecognizePresentation, examination findings, and the characteristic lab picture

Hyperaldosteronism, primary and secondary

The commonest secondary cause of hypertension, and routinely missed because the potassium is often normal.

What aldosterone does, so you can predict the syndrome

Aldosterone acts on the principal cells of the distal nephron: it upregulates the Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase and the epithelial sodium channel, so sodium is retained, water follows, and potassium and hydrogen ions are excreted. Predict from that: hypertension from volume expansion, hypokalemia, and a metabolic alkalosis. Sodium is often near-normal because water is retained alongside it. It is normally released in response to angiotensin II (that is, to low renal perfusion or low blood pressure) and to high serum potassium; ACTH contributes to a minor extent.

Primary — the adrenal is autonomous
Aldosterone is high and renin is suppressed, because the volume expansion shuts the renin–angiotensin system off and the gland ignores it anyway. Causes: bilateral adrenocortical hyperplasia (idiopathic hyperaldosteronism, the commonest), an aldosterone-producing adenoma — Conn syndrome, rarely an adrenocortical carcinoma, and the familial forms including glucocorticoid-remediable aldosteronism. Symptoms are those of hypertension plus those of hypokalemia — weakness, paresthesias, tetany, visual disturbance, polyuria — and in a great many patients, nothing at all.
Secondary — the signal is driving it
Aldosterone is high and renin is high too, because the adrenal is responding correctly to an abnormal stimulus. Anything that reduces renal perfusion will do it: renal artery stenosis or atheroma, renal vasoconstriction as in accelerated hypertension, and the edematous states — heart failure, cirrhosis with ascites, nephrotic syndrome. Renin is the single value that separates the two, which is why the screening test is a ratio and not an aldosterone level.
Who to screen — a high index of suspicion

Test for primary aldosteronism in: sustained blood pressure above 150/100 on three measurements on three different days; hypertension above 140/90 resistant to three agents; controlled hypertension requiring four or more agents; hypertension with hypokalemia — whether spontaneous or diuretic-induced; hypertension with an adrenal incidentaloma; hypertension with obstructive sleep apnea; hypertension with a family history of early-onset hypertension or of stroke before 40; and hypertension in anyone with a first-degree relative who has primary aldosteronism. Note that normal potassium does not exclude it — hypokalemia is present in a minority.

Cushing syndrome and Cushing disease

Two terms that are not interchangeable, and the distinction is examinable.

Get the vocabulary right first

Cushing syndrome is the whole clinical picture produced by inappropriately high exposure to glucocorticoid, from any source. Cushing disease is the specific subset caused by an ACTH-secreting pituitary adenoma. Every Cushing disease is a Cushing syndrome; most Cushing syndrome is not Cushing disease. And the commonest cause of the syndrome overall is not a tumour at all — it is exogenous glucocorticoid therapy.

The features, and the mechanism behind each
Central obesity with thin extremities — cortisol is catabolic and an insulin antagonist: it mobilizes peripheral fat and breaks down muscle while promoting visceral fat deposition. Thin, easily bruised skin and wide purple striae — impaired collagen synthesis and abnormal wound healing. Facial plethora — dilated facial capillaries under thinned skin. Proximal muscle weakness — the same catabolism. Secondary hypertension — cortisol potentiates the vasoconstrictor effect of catecholamines, and at high concentrations it also spills onto the mineralocorticoid receptor. Hyperglycemia and diabetes, hypertriglyceridemia — insulin resistance. Osteoporosis, immunosuppression, mood disturbance, and hypogonadism, because cortisol suppresses gonadotropins.
In children the presentation is different
The single most useful pediatric sign is growth failure with weight gain — a child crossing height percentiles downward while crossing weight percentiles upward. Almost nothing else does that. Obesity alone in a child is usually exogenous and comes with accelerated growth; it is the combination that should prompt testing.
When to raise your index of suspicion

Test people with features unusual for their age — hypertension, osteoporosis, type 2 diabetes, or thin skin in a young adult; people with multiple and progressive features rather than one in isolation; children with a falling height percentile and a rising weight percentile; and people with an adrenal incidentaloma. The reason for the threshold is that individual features — obesity, hypertension, glucose intolerance — are extremely common in the general population and testing everybody produces mostly false positives.

Primary adrenal insufficiency

Described by Thomas Addison in 1855. Progressive destruction of the adrenal cortex — so both cortisol and aldosterone are lost.

Predict every symptom from the two missing hormones

Missing cortisol: fatigue, anorexia, weight loss, nausea and vomiting, abdominal pain, apathy and confusion, hypoglycemia, and an inability to withstand physiological stress. Missing aldosterone: sodium wasting → hyponatremia, hypovolemia, postural hypotension, tachycardia, salt craving, and — because potassium is no longer excreted — hyperkalemia, often with a mild metabolic acidosis. The one finding that is not a deficiency at all: hyperpigmentation, most obvious in palmar creases, buccal mucosa, scars, and pressure areas. It is caused by the excess ACTH and related POMC-derived peptides, and it is the physical sign that tells you the problem is primary rather than central. In neonates, prolonged jaundice belongs on the list too.

Causes — autoimmune leads in high-income countries
Autoimmune adrenalitis (Addison disease), with 21-hydroxylase antibodies, often as part of an autoimmune polyglandular syndrome — which is why a patient recently started on levothyroxine who then deteriorates deserves a second look. Infection — tuberculosis, which remains the leading cause worldwide; disseminated fungal disease; HIV-associated disease; and Waterhouse-Friderichsen syndrome. Infiltration — amyloidosis, sarcoidosis, hemochromatosis, metastases. Genetic — congenital adrenal hypoplasia (DAX1/NR0B1, SF1/NR5A1, ACTH-receptor mutations) and adrenoleukodystrophy. Metabolic and drug-related — congenital adrenal hyperplasia, and drugs that inhibit steroidogenesis.
Adrenoleukodystrophy — worth knowing separately
An X-linked peroxisomal disorder (ABCD1) in which very long chain fatty acids accumulate in the brain, adrenal cortex, and testicular Leydig cells. It can present as isolated adrenal insufficiency years before neurological disease, so every boy with primary adrenal insufficiency and no antibodies needs a VLCFA level. It was added to the US Recommended Uniform Screening Panel in 2016, so most children in the United States are now screened at birth.
The picture
Overwhelming bacterial sepsis — classically Neisseria meningitidis — with rapidly progressive hypotension and shock, disseminated intravascular coagulation with purpura, and massive bilateral adrenal hemorrhage producing acute adrenocortical insufficiency. More common in children. If the infection is not treated within hours, death follows in hours to days.
The pathology
The mechanism of the hemorrhage is not settled; bacterial seeding of small adrenal vessels with endotoxin-induced vasculitis is the usual explanation. Macroscopically the glands are hemorrhagic sacs and the cortical architecture is indiscernible.
What you actually do
Antibiotics and resuscitation immediately, and stress-dose hydrocortisone without waiting for a cortisol result. This is the classic acute presentation of primary adrenal failure and the reason "treat first, confirm later" is the rule in adrenal crisis.

Central adrenal insufficiency — secondary and tertiary

What is different, and why
Any disorder of the pituitary or hypothalamus that reduces ACTH gives low ACTH with low cortisol. The critical difference from primary disease is that aldosterone is normal, because the zona glomerulosa answers to the renin–angiotensin system and to potassium, not to ACTH. Therefore: no hyperkalemia, no salt craving, no mineralocorticoid replacement needed. And no excess ACTH means no hyperpigmentation. Hyponatremia can still occur, because cortisol normally restrains vasopressin release.
Causes
Withdrawal of exogenous glucocorticoid therapy — by a wide margin the commonest cause of central adrenal insufficiency, and the reason chronic steroids are tapered rather than stopped. Hypopituitarism from any cause — tumour, surgery, radiation, apoplexy, infiltration. Hypothalamic disease — tumour, radiation, surgery (this is the tertiary form). Isolated ACTH deficiency, and defects in POMC, which characteristically also give red hair and early obesity.
Primary versus central at a glance

Both: low cortisol, fatigue, weight loss, hypoglycemia, hyponatremia. Primary only: high ACTH, hyperpigmentation, low aldosterone with high renin, hyperkalemia, salt craving, and a need for fludrocortisone. Central only: low ACTH, other pituitary hormone deficits, and often a history of exogenous steroids.

Congenital adrenal hyperplasia

A group of autosomal recessive loss-of-function disorders of the steroidogenic enzymes. The mechanism is derived step by step on the steroid synthesis page; here it is the clinical face.

The shape of the disease

An enzyme in the cortisol pathway is deficient → cortisol falls → ACTH rises → the adrenals become bilaterally hyperplastic, up to 10–15 times normal weight → precursors above the block accumulate and are shunted into whichever branch is still open. In the commonest form that branch is the androgens. 21-hydroxylase deficiency accounts for 90–95% of cases. In the severe form both cortisol and aldosterone are lost, so infants present with adrenal and salt-wasting crisis: poor feeding, vomiting, failure to thrive, dehydration, hyponatremia, hyperkalemia, hypoglycemia, and shock, typically at 1 to 3 weeks of age. 46,XX infants have atypical genitalia; 46,XY infants may look entirely normal — which is precisely why the newborn screen exists.

The two hypertensive forms, and why the aldosterone value looks wrong

In both 11β-hydroxylase and 17α-hydroxylase deficiency, measured aldosterone is low but the patient is hypertensive and hypokalemic. That is not a contradiction: 11-deoxycorticosterone accumulates above the block, and DOC is a weak mineralocorticoid present in large enough quantities to act like a strong one. The excess mineralocorticoid activity then suppresses renin, and low renin suppresses aldosterone. Hypertension in a child with CAH means DOC, and it is the finding that separates these two from 21-hydroxylase deficiency at the bedside.

Working up virilization in a child

A common presentation with a short and orderly differential.

The differential, roughly in order
Idiopathic — the commonest. Premature adrenarche — early pubic and axillary hair from early maturation of the zona reticularis, without other pubertal signs. Insulin resistance and PCOS. Congenital adrenal hyperplasia, including the non-classic form. An adrenal or gonadal tumour — suspect this when onset is rapid, virilization is severe, or the androgen levels are extreme. Iatrogenic androgen exposure, which is easy to miss and free to ask about.
First-line tests, and reading them
17-hydroxyprogesterone (early morning) — high points to CAH. DHEA-S — the adrenal androgen marker; a markedly raised value, of the order of ten times the upper limit, points to an adrenal tumour rather than hyperplasia. Androstenedione and testosterone — a very high testosterone with a normal DHEA-S points to a gonadal source. Add bone age, and imaging directed by the biochemistry.

The incidentaloma — two questions, always both

Is it functioning? Screen every adrenal incidentaloma with a 1 mg overnight dexamethasone suppression test and plasma or urinary metanephrines, and add an aldosterone-to-renin ratio if the patient is hypertensive or hypokalemic. Is it malignant? Judge from size and imaging phenotype — a small, homogeneous, lipid-rich lesion with low attenuation on unenhanced CT behaves like an adenoma, whereas a large, heterogeneous, irregular lesion does not. Biopsy is not the first move, and must never be done before pheochromocytoma has been excluded biochemically.

Pheochromocytoma and paraganglioma

A catecholamine-secreting tumour of the adrenal medulla. Outside the medulla the same tumour is called a paraganglioma.

The presentation
The classic triad is headache, diaphoresis, and palpitations, occurring in episodes that wax and wane. The commonest single finding is hypertension — which may be sustained, paroxysmal, or both, and is often severe. Add pallor, tremor, anxiety, and hyperglycemia. A young or middle-aged patient with resistant or episodic hypertension is the person to think of it in.
Paraganglioma and the genetics
Paragangliomas arise from extra-adrenal chromaffin tissue and are strongly associated with mutations of the succinate dehydrogenase (SDHx) genes, of which SDHB carries the highest risk of metastatic disease. Every patient should be referred for genetic testing — a large minority of these tumours are germline — and the syndromes to know are MEN2A and MEN2B (RET), von Hippel-Lindau (VHL), neurofibromatosis type 1 (NF1), and the hereditary paraganglioma syndromes (SDHx). Up to 20–25% become metastatic, so surveillance is lifelong.
One correction worth making against the slide set

The lecture lists Sturge-Weber syndrome among the syndromic associations of pheochromocytoma. The associations consistently reported in the literature are MEN2, VHL, NF1, and the SDHx paraganglioma syndromes; Sturge-Weber is not an established one. Learn the four — they are the ones a question will test, and they are the ones that determine which gene panel is sent.

The multiple endocrine neoplasia syndromes

All three are autosomal dominant. Two genes cover all of them: MEN1 for type 1, RET for both type 2 syndromes.

A distinction the slide set blurs

The slides place Hirschsprung disease in the MEN2B column alongside intestinal ganglioneuromas. The published association is the other way round: Hirschsprung disease goes with MEN2A (typically RET exon 10 variants), while diffuse intestinal ganglioneuromatosis is the MEN2B feature. The two are opposite lesions of the enteric nervous system — absent ganglion cells in Hirschsprung, excess ganglion cells in ganglioneuromatosis — which is a good way to keep them apart. Cutaneous lichen amyloidosis is a MEN2A feature (classically codon 634).

What a MEN2 diagnosis obliges you to do

Medullary thyroid carcinoma arises from parafollicular C cells and secretes calcitonin, which is its tumour marker. Because penetrance approaches 100%, RET carriers are offered prophylactic thyroidectomy, timed by the specific variant's risk. Pheochromocytoma requires lifelong surveillance with plasma or urinary metanephrines, and must be excluded and treated before any other surgery — operating on an unblocked pheochromocytoma can precipitate a hypertensive crisis. Hyperparathyroidism in MEN2A is followed with calcium and PTH.

Two numbers, and a caveat about them

The slide set gives >2 µg/dL as inadequate suppression on the overnight dexamethasone test. The threshold in wide use, including in the Endocrine Society guidance, is 1.8 µg/dL (50 nmol/L), chosen deliberately to favour sensitivity — it will call some normal people abnormal, which is the correct trade-off for a screening test that is always followed by a second one. Similarly, the 18 µg/dL post-cosyntropin threshold comes from older cortisol assays; modern specific assays read lower and many centres now use a cut-off in the 14–15 µg/dL range. Know the classic numbers, and know that they are assay-dependent.

ActManagement, by disorder and by level of the lesion

Management

Objective 3. Notice how often the level of the lesion — gland, pituitary, or hypothalamus — changes the treatment rather than just the label.

Unilateral — an adenoma
Laparoscopic adrenalectomy. These tumours are usually under 2 cm. Surgery reliably corrects the hypokalemia and improves, though does not always cure, the hypertension.
Bilateral hyperplasia
Mineralocorticoid receptor antagonist — spironolactone or eplerenone. Removing one of two overactive glands does not solve a bilateral problem.
Glucocorticoid-remediable aldosteronism
A low-dose glucocorticoid to suppress the ACTH drive, with or without an MRA. Very rare, but it is the one form where the treatment follows directly from the molecular lesion — see the steroid page.
Secondary hyperaldosteronism
Treat the cause of the renal hypoperfusion — revascularize the stenosis, manage the heart failure or the cirrhosis. Blocking aldosterone alone addresses a symptom of the real problem.
Pituitary adenoma — Cushing disease
Transsphenoidal resection is first line, with radiation for residual or recurrent disease.
Primary adrenal disease
Unilateral or bilateral adrenalectomy depending on the lesion. After bilateral adrenalectomy the patient needs lifelong glucocorticoid and mineralocorticoid replacement.
Ectopic ACTH
Excise the tumour where that is possible.
Medical therapy
For recurrence, inoperable disease, or to control severe hypercortisolism before surgery: steroidogenesis inhibitors (ketoconazole, metyrapone, osilodrostat), pasireotide acting on the pituitary tumour, and mifepristone, a glucocorticoid receptor antagonist used mainly for the hyperglycemia.
Primary
Glucocorticoid and mineralocorticoid. Hydrocortisone in divided doses mimicking the diurnal rhythm, plus fludrocortisone because the zona glomerulosa has been destroyed along with everything else. Infants and small children also need sodium chloride supplementation.
Secondary and tertiary
Glucocorticoid only. No fludrocortisone is needed, because the adrenal gland and the renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system are both intact — the gland is simply unstimulated. This single difference is the practical reason localization matters.
Non-negotiable in both
Stress dosing — two to three times the usual dose during febrile illness, and parenteral hydrocortisone for vomiting, trauma, or surgery. An emergency injection kit, written sick-day rules, and medical alert identification. Patients die of untreated crises, not of the underlying diagnosis.
The regimen
Hydrocortisone for glucocorticoid replacement — preferred in growing children because its short half-life is least suppressive of growth, and it carries useful mineralocorticoid activity at higher doses. Fludrocortisone for the mineralocorticoid arm — note there is no intravenous form, which matters in a vomiting child. Salt supplementation in infancy.
The therapeutic target is a balance
Enough glucocorticoid to prevent crisis and suppress the androgen drive, not so much that it stunts growth and causes iatrogenic Cushing syndrome. Under-treatment lets androgens advance bone age and shorten adult height; over-treatment does the same thing by a different route. Monitor growth velocity, bone age, 17-OHP, and androstenedione.
Alpha blockade first
Normalize the blood pressure before anything else, with an α-adrenergic blocker — phenoxybenzamine, or a selective α1 agent such as doxazosin or prazosin — started well ahead of surgery, together with liberal salt and fluid intake to re-expand the contracted plasma volume.
Never a beta blocker alone
Blocking β receptors while α receptors remain unopposed removes β2-mediated vasodilation and leaves pure α-mediated vasoconstriction — a hypertensive crisis. A beta blocker may be added after adequate α blockade to control reflex tachycardia, never before.
Then surgery
Surgical excision, which is a high-risk procedure requiring an experienced surgical and anesthetic team because manipulating the tumour releases catecholamines. Afterwards: lifelong biochemical surveillance, because a fifth to a quarter of these tumours behave malignantly.

Adrenal crisis — the one to know cold

Treat first, confirm afterwards

An unwell patient with hypotension unresponsive to fluids, hyponatremia, hyperkalemia, and hypoglycemia — particularly a neonate in the second or third week of life, a patient with known adrenal insufficiency who has become ill, or anyone who has recently stopped long-term steroids — is in adrenal crisis until proved otherwise. Draw a cortisol and an ACTH if you can do it in seconds, then give hydrocortisone. Do not wait for the result. Intravenous hydrocortisone — 100 mg in an adult, weight- or surface-area-based in a child — then intravenous fluid resuscitation with saline containing dextrose, correction of the hypoglycemia, treatment of the precipitating illness, and cardiac monitoring for the hyperkalemia. At stress doses hydrocortisone has enough mineralocorticoid activity that fludrocortisone is not needed acutely — which is convenient, since it only exists as a tablet.

RetrieveCheck yourself before you leave the page

Retrieval prompts

Because ACTH is low, and because aldosterone is not an ACTH-dependent hormone. The zona glomerulosa answers to angiotensin II and potassium, both of which are intact, so aldosterone is preserved and potassium is handled normally — hence no hyperkalemia and no need for fludrocortisone. Hyperpigmentation requires excess ACTH and related POMC peptides acting on melanocortin receptors in skin; with ACTH low there is nothing to drive it. The two findings are the fastest bedside way to say primary or central.

Because CT shows anatomy and the question is function. Non-functioning adrenal nodules are common and become commoner with age, so the visible nodule may be an innocent bystander while the contralateral gland — or both glands — is the source. CT also misses small aldosterone-producing adenomas altogether. Removing the wrong gland leaves the patient with persistent hyperaldosteronism and one fewer adrenal. CT is still done in everyone, but for a different reason: to exclude a large mass suspicious for adrenocortical carcinoma.

21-hydroxylase deficiency and 11β-hydroxylase deficiency. The block in 21-hydroxylase deficiency sits above 11-deoxycorticosterone, so no mineralocorticoid of any kind is made and the child salt-wastes. The block in 11β-hydroxylase deficiency sits one step below DOC, so DOC accumulates; it is a weak mineralocorticoid, but in that quantity it retains sodium, raises blood pressure, and wastes potassium. Both children virilize, because in both the accumulated precursor is shunted into the androgen branch.

Because secretion is episodic but metabolism is continuous. The tumour metabolizes catecholamines to metanephrines inside the tumour cells, around the clock, and leaks the metabolites steadily. So a catecholamine level drawn between paroxysms can be normal, whereas metanephrines stay elevated. That is what gives plasma free metanephrines their very high sensitivity — the property you want in a screening test for a tumour you cannot afford to miss.

MEN2A — hyperparathyroidism plus a pheochromocytoma, both from a RET mutation, with medullary thyroid carcinoma to be sought urgently. MEN1 includes hyperparathyroidism but not pheochromocytoma; MEN2B includes pheochromocytoma but not hyperparathyroidism. Before any operation the pheochromocytoma must be diagnosed biochemically and the patient α-blocked and volume-repleted. Anesthesia in an unprepared patient can precipitate a fatal hypertensive crisis, and the thyroid surgery this patient will eventually need must wait behind the adrenal.
