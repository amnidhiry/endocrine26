Adrenal Gland Disorders

Four hormones come out of this gland — aldosterone, cortisol, adrenal androgens, and catecholamines — and each one can be present in excess or absent. That gives you eight boxes, and nearly every disorder in this session lives in one of them. Work out which hormone is wrong and in which direction, then ask whether the fault is in the gland or in the signal driving it. Everything else is detail.

Orientation: four hormones, two control systems

The three objectives for this session

1. Match symptoms, signs, and lab findings to the disorder — every disorder section below. 2. Choose the first-line diagnostic test — all of them in one table. 3. Manage primary, secondary, and tertiary causes — management, plus adrenal crisis, the one to know cold.

Cortex — three zones
  • Glomerulosa → aldosterone, answering to angiotensin II and potassium.
  • Fasciculata → cortisol, answering to ACTH.
  • Reticularis → DHEA and androstenedione, also ACTH-responsive.

The synthetic pathway itself is worked through on the steroid synthesis page.

Medulla — a different organ
Neural-crest-derived chromaffin cells making catecholamines. Not part of the steroid story at all, not under ACTH control, and the source of pheochromocytoma. It is inside the same capsule; that is the whole relationship.
The two questions to ask every time
Which hormone, and in which direction? That names the syndrome. Gland or signal? That names the level — primary means the gland, secondary means the pituitary, tertiary means the hypothalamus, and for aldosterone "secondary" means the renin–angiotensin system is driving it.

Hyperaldosteronism, primary and secondary

The commonest secondary cause of hypertension, and routinely missed because the potassium is often normal.

Original diagram. Both columns have high aldosterone — everything below the join is identical, which is why the presentations are indistinguishable at the bedside and why renin is the value that names the disease. Read the two boxes at the top against each other: in primary disease the gland ignores the signal and the resulting volume expansion suppresses renin, so the two move in opposite directions; in secondary disease the signal is the problem, so both are high. Aldosterone's normal stimuli are angiotensin II and high serum potassium, with ACTH contributing slightly.

Primary — the causes, in order
  • Bilateral adrenocortical hyperplasia (idiopathic) — the commonest.
  • An aldosterone-producing adenoma — Conn syndrome.
  • Adrenocortical carcinoma — rare.
  • Familial forms, notably glucocorticoid-remediable aldosteronism.

The point that catches people out: a great many patients have no symptoms at all. This is the commonest secondary cause of hypertension and it is found by screening, not by complaint.

Secondary — anything that drops renal perfusion
  • Obstructive renal artery disease — atheroma or stenosis.
  • Renal vasoconstriction, as in accelerated hypertension.
  • The edematous states — heart failure, cirrhosis with ascites, nephrotic syndrome.

In every one of them the adrenal is behaving correctly, which is why treatment means fixing the cause rather than blocking the hormone.

Who to screen

Sustained BP above 150/100 on three measurements on three different days, BP above 140/90 resistant to three agents, controlled BP needing four or more agents, hypertension with hypokalemia, hypertension with an adrenal incidentaloma, and hypertension with a family history of early-onset hypertension, stroke before 40, or a first-degree relative with primary aldosteronism. Normal potassium does not exclude it: hypokalemia is present in only a minority.

Cushing syndrome and Cushing disease

Two terms that are not interchangeable, and the distinction is examinable.

Get the vocabulary right first

Cushing syndrome is the clinical picture of excess glucocorticoid exposure from any source. Cushing disease is the subset caused by an ACTH-secreting pituitary adenoma. Every Cushing disease is a Cushing syndrome; most Cushing syndrome is not Cushing disease — the commonest cause overall is exogenous glucocorticoid therapy.

Illustration via News-Medical (Shutterstock 286083608). Two label translations worth making, because the exam uses the other words: "red and round face" is moon facies with plethora, and "skin ulcers" is the impaired wound healing that goes with thin skin. Note also that the figure puts fat on the abdomen and the back of the neck while the limbs stay thin — that redistribution, rather than weight gain as such, is what makes the picture Cushingoid.

Four mechanisms generate every finding on that figure
  • Cortisol is catabolic and an insulin antagonist — it breaks down peripheral muscle and fat while depositing visceral and dorsocervical fat, which accounts for the central obesity with thin extremities, the proximal muscle wasting, and the hyperglycemia and hypertriglyceridemia.
  • It impairs collagen synthesis — thin, easily bruised skin, wide purple striae, poor wound healing, and the facial plethora of dilated capillaries under thinned skin.
  • It potentiates catecholamine vasoconstriction and at high concentrations spills onto the mineralocorticoid receptor — hypertension, and the cardiac hypertrophy behind it.
  • It suppresses gonadotropins — amenorrhea and hirsutism in women, erectile dysfunction in men.

Osteoporosis, immunosuppression, and mood disturbance complete the list.

In children the presentation is different
The most useful pediatric sign is growth failure with weight gain — a child crossing height percentiles downward while crossing weight percentiles upward. Almost nothing else does that. Simple obesity comes with accelerated growth.
When to test

Features unusual for the age — hypertension, osteoporosis, type 2 diabetes, or thin skin in a young adult; multiple, progressive features rather than one in isolation; a child with a falling height and rising weight percentile; and an adrenal incidentaloma. Any single feature is too common in the population to justify testing on its own.

Primary adrenal insufficiency (Addison's disease)

Described by Thomas Addison in 1855. Progressive destruction of the adrenal cortex — so both cortisol and aldosterone are lost.

Visual map by Priyanga Singh, Creative Med Doses (©2019). The letters of ADDISON carry the presentation. The one line to take from it is the footnote: the commonest presentation is hyponatremia with hyperpigmentation. Note the mechanism it draws explicitly — POMC → ↑MSH → dark skin — which is why the pigment appears and why it appears only in primary disease. The figure also flags the ACTH stimulation test → low cortisol as the confirmatory step.

Sort the findings by which hormone is missing

The mnemonic gives you the list; this gives you the reason, and the reason is what a question tests. Missing cortisol explains the fatigue, weight loss, anorexia, abdominal pain and vomiting, hypoglycemia, and the inability to withstand physiological stress. Missing aldosterone explains the rest: sodium wasting gives hyponatremia, hypovolemia, postural hypotension and salt craving, and potassium no longer being excreted gives hyperkalemia with a mild metabolic acidosis. That second group is the group central disease does not have. Hyperpigmentation is neither — it is a consequence of the excess ACTH, so it too is unique to primary disease. In neonates, add prolonged jaundice.

Causes — autoimmune leads in high-income countries
  • Autoimmune adrenalitis — the commonest cause at roughly 80%, with 21-hydroxylase antibodies, often part of an autoimmune polyglandular syndrome; so a patient recently started on levothyroxine who then deteriorates deserves a second look.
  • Infection — tuberculosis (the leading cause worldwide), disseminated fungal disease, HIV, and Waterhouse-Friderichsen syndrome.
  • Infiltration — amyloidosis, sarcoidosis, hemochromatosis, metastases.
  • Genetic — congenital adrenal hypoplasia (DAX1, SF1, ACTH-receptor mutations) and adrenoleukodystrophy.
  • Other — congenital adrenal hyperplasia, and drugs that inhibit steroidogenesis.
Adrenoleukodystrophy
An X-linked peroxisomal disorder in which very long chain fatty acids accumulate in the brain, adrenal cortex, and Leydig cells. It can present as isolated adrenal insufficiency years before the neurological disease, so every boy with primary adrenal insufficiency and negative antibodies needs a VLCFA level. It is on the recommended newborn screen.
The picture
Overwhelming bacterial sepsis — classically Neisseria meningitidis — with rapidly progressive hypotension and shock, DIC with purpura, and massive bilateral adrenal hemorrhage producing acute adrenocortical insufficiency. More common in children, and fatal within hours to days if untreated.
The pathology
Bacterial seeding of small adrenal vessels with endotoxin-induced vasculitis. The glands become hemorrhagic sacs in which the cortical architecture is indiscernible.
What you do
Antibiotics and resuscitation immediately, plus stress-dose hydrocortisone without waiting for a cortisol result — the reason "treat first, confirm later" is the rule in adrenal crisis.

Central adrenal insufficiency — secondary and tertiary

What is different, and why
Any pituitary or hypothalamic disorder that reduces ACTH gives low ACTH with low cortisol. The critical difference from primary disease is that aldosterone is normal, because the zona glomerulosa answers to the renin–angiotensin system and potassium, not to ACTH. So: no hyperkalemia, no salt craving, no mineralocorticoid replacement. And no excess ACTH means no hyperpigmentation. Hyponatremia can still occur, because cortisol normally restrains vasopressin release.
Causes
  • Withdrawal of exogenous glucocorticoid — by a wide margin the commonest cause, and the reason chronic steroids are tapered rather than stopped.
  • Hypopituitarism from any cause — tumour, surgery, radiation, apoplexy, infiltration.
  • Hypothalamic disease — the tertiary form.
  • Isolated ACTH deficiency or a POMC defect.
Primary versus central at a glance

Both: low cortisol, fatigue, weight loss, hypoglycemia, hyponatremia. Primary only: high ACTH, hyperpigmentation, low aldosterone with high renin, hyperkalemia, salt craving, and a need for fludrocortisone. Central only: low ACTH, other pituitary hormone deficits, and often a history of exogenous steroids.

Congenital adrenal hyperplasia

A group of autosomal recessive loss-of-function disorders of the steroidogenic enzymes. The mechanism is derived step by step on the steroid synthesis page; here it is the clinical face.

The shape of the disease

An enzyme in the cortisol pathway is deficient → cortisol falls → ACTH rises → the adrenals become bilaterally hyperplastic, up to 10–15 times normal weight → precursors above the block accumulate and are shunted into whichever branch is still open, usually the androgens. 21-hydroxylase deficiency accounts for 90–95% of cases. In the severe form both cortisol and aldosterone are lost, so infants present in salt-wasting adrenal crisis at 1 to 3 weeks of age: poor feeding, vomiting, failure to thrive, dehydration, hyponatremia, hyperkalemia, hypoglycemia, shock. 46,XX infants have atypical genitalia; 46,XY infants may look entirely normal — which is why the newborn screen exists.

The two hypertensive forms, and why the aldosterone looks wrong

In both 11β-hydroxylase and 17α-hydroxylase deficiency, aldosterone is low but the patient is hypertensive and hypokalemic. Not a contradiction: 11-deoxycorticosterone accumulates above the block, and DOC is a weak mineralocorticoid present in large enough quantities to act like a strong one — which then suppresses renin, and so aldosterone. Hypertension in a child with CAH means DOC, and it is what separates these two from 21-hydroxylase deficiency at the bedside.

Working up virilization in a child

A common presentation with a short and orderly differential.

The differential, roughly in order
  • Idiopathic — the commonest.
  • Premature adrenarche — early pubic and axillary hair without other pubertal signs.
  • Insulin resistance and PCOS.
  • Congenital adrenal hyperplasia, including the non-classic form.
  • An adrenal or gonadal tumour — suspect when onset is rapid, virilization severe, or androgen levels extreme.
  • Iatrogenic androgen exposure.
First-line tests, and reading them
  • 17-hydroxyprogesterone (early morning) — high points to CAH.
  • DHEA-S — the adrenal androgen marker; a markedly raised value, of the order of ten times the upper limit, points to an adrenal tumour rather than hyperplasia.
  • Androstenedione and testosterone — a very high testosterone with a normal DHEA-S points to a gonadal source.

Add bone age, and imaging directed by the biochemistry.

Treatment, and the incidental mass

Both are treated by excision. For a mass found incidentally, the two questions are is it functioning — screen with a dexamethasone suppression test and metanephrines — and is it malignant, judged on size and imaging phenotype. Never biopsy before pheochromocytoma has been excluded biochemically.

Pheochromocytoma and paraganglioma

A catecholamine-secreting tumour of the adrenal medulla. Outside the medulla the same tumour is called a paraganglioma.

The presentation
  • The classic triad is headache, diaphoresis, and palpitations, in episodes that wax and wane.
  • The commonest single finding is hypertension — sustained, paroxysmal, or both, and often severe.
  • Then pallor, tremor, anxiety, and hyperglycemia.

Think of it in a young or middle-aged patient with resistant or episodic hypertension.

Paraganglioma and the genetics
  • Paragangliomas arise from extra-adrenal chromaffin tissue.
  • Refer every patient for genetic testing — a large minority are germline. The four syndromes to know: MEN2A and MEN2B (RET), von Hippel-Lindau (VHL), neurofibromatosis type 1 (NF1), and the hereditary paraganglioma syndromes (SDHx).
  • Up to 20–25% become metastatic, so surveillance is lifelong.

Note: the slides also list Sturge-Weber, which is not an established association — learn the four.

The multiple endocrine neoplasia syndromes

All three are autosomal dominant. Two genes cover all of them: MEN1 for type 1, RET for both type 2 syndromes.

A distinction the slide set blurs

The slides put Hirschsprung disease in the MEN2B column. The published association is the other way round: Hirschsprung goes with MEN2A, while diffuse intestinal ganglioneuromatosis is the MEN2B feature. They are opposite lesions of the enteric nervous system — absent ganglion cells versus excess — which is the way to keep them apart. Cutaneous lichen amyloidosis is MEN2A.

What a MEN2 diagnosis obliges you to do

Medullary thyroid carcinoma arises from parafollicular C cells and secretes calcitonin, its tumour marker; because penetrance approaches 100%, RET carriers are offered prophylactic thyroidectomy. Pheochromocytoma needs lifelong metanephrine surveillance and must be excluded and treated before any other surgery — operating on an unblocked pheochromocytoma can precipitate a hypertensive crisis. Hyperparathyroidism in MEN2A is followed with calcium and PTH.

Two numbers worth pinning down

Inadequate suppression on the overnight dexamethasone test: the slides say >2 µg/dL, the Endocrine Society >1.8 µg/dL — set low on purpose, because a screening test should favour sensitivity and is always followed by a second one. Post-cosyntropin, a peak below 18 µg/dL is the classic diagnostic threshold. Both are assay-dependent.

ActManagement, by disorder and by level of the lesion

Management

Objective 3. Notice how often the level of the lesion — gland, pituitary, or hypothalamus — changes the treatment rather than just the label.

Unilateral — an adenoma
Laparoscopic adrenalectomy. Reliably corrects the hypokalemia and improves, though does not always cure, the hypertension.
Bilateral hyperplasia
Mineralocorticoid receptor antagonist — spironolactone or eplerenone. Removing one of two overactive glands does not solve a bilateral problem.
Glucocorticoid-remediable aldosteronism
A low-dose glucocorticoid to suppress the ACTH drive, with or without an MRA — see the steroid page.
Secondary hyperaldosteronism
Treat the cause of the renal hypoperfusion — revascularize the stenosis, manage the heart failure or cirrhosis. Blocking aldosterone alone treats a symptom.
Pituitary adenoma — Cushing disease
Transsphenoidal resection is first line, with radiation for residual or recurrent disease.
Primary adrenal disease
Unilateral or bilateral adrenalectomy depending on the lesion. After bilateral adrenalectomy, lifelong glucocorticoid and mineralocorticoid replacement.
Ectopic ACTH
Excise the tumour where that is possible.
Medical therapy

For recurrence, inoperable disease, or severe hypercortisolism before surgery:

  • Steroidogenesis inhibitors — ketoconazole, metyrapone, osilodrostat.
  • Pasireotide, acting on the pituitary tumour.
  • Mifepristone, a glucocorticoid receptor antagonist.
Primary
Glucocorticoid and mineralocorticoid. Hydrocortisone in divided doses mimicking the diurnal rhythm, plus fludrocortisone because the zona glomerulosa was destroyed too. Infants and small children also need sodium chloride supplementation.
Secondary and tertiary
Glucocorticoid only. No fludrocortisone — the gland and the renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system are intact, just unstimulated. This single difference is the practical reason localization matters.
Non-negotiable in both
  • Stress dosing — two to three times the usual dose in febrile illness, and parenteral hydrocortisone for vomiting, trauma, or surgery.
  • An emergency injection kit and written sick-day rules.
  • Medical alert identification.

Patients die of untreated crises, not of the diagnosis.

The regimen
  • Hydrocortisone — preferred in growing children because its short half-life is least suppressive of growth.
  • Fludrocortisone for the mineralocorticoid arm; note there is no intravenous form, which matters in a vomiting child.
  • Salt supplementation in infancy.
The target is a balance
Enough glucocorticoid to prevent crisis and suppress the androgen drive, not so much that it stunts growth and causes iatrogenic Cushing syndrome. Under-treatment lets androgens advance bone age and shorten adult height; over-treatment shortens it by a different route. Monitor growth velocity, bone age, 17-OHP, and androstenedione.
Alpha blockade first
Normalize the blood pressure before anything else, with an α-adrenergic blocker — phenoxybenzamine, or a selective α1 agent such as doxazosin — started well ahead of surgery, with liberal salt and fluid to re-expand the contracted plasma volume.
Never a beta blocker alone
Blocking β while α is unopposed removes β2-mediated vasodilation and leaves pure α-mediated vasoconstriction — a hypertensive crisis. A beta blocker is added after adequate α blockade to control reflex tachycardia, never before.
Then surgery
Surgical excision by an experienced team, because manipulating the tumour releases catecholamines. Afterwards, lifelong biochemical surveillance.

Adrenal crisis — the one to know cold

Treat first, confirm afterwards

An unwell patient with hypotension unresponsive to fluids, hyponatremia, hyperkalemia, and hypoglycemia — particularly a neonate in the second or third week of life, a known adrenal-insufficient patient who has become ill, or anyone who recently stopped long-term steroids — is in adrenal crisis until proved otherwise. Draw cortisol and ACTH only if it takes seconds, then give hydrocortisone. Do not wait for the result. IV hydrocortisone (100 mg in an adult, weight-based in a child), saline with dextrose, correction of the hypoglycemia, treatment of the precipitating illness, and cardiac monitoring for the hyperkalemia. At stress doses hydrocortisone has enough mineralocorticoid activity that fludrocortisone is not needed acutely — convenient, since it only exists as a tablet.

RetrieveCheck yourself before you leave the page

Retrieval prompts

Because ACTH is low, and because aldosterone is not an ACTH-dependent hormone. The zona glomerulosa answers to angiotensin II and potassium, both of which are intact, so aldosterone is preserved and potassium is handled normally — hence no hyperkalemia and no need for fludrocortisone. Hyperpigmentation requires excess ACTH and related POMC peptides acting on melanocortin receptors in skin; with ACTH low there is nothing to drive it. The two findings are the fastest bedside way to say primary or central.

Because CT shows anatomy and the question is function. Non-functioning adrenal nodules are common and become commoner with age, so the visible nodule may be an innocent bystander while the contralateral gland — or both glands — is the source. CT also misses small aldosterone-producing adenomas altogether. Removing the wrong gland leaves the patient with persistent hyperaldosteronism and one fewer adrenal. CT is still done in everyone, but for a different reason: to exclude a large mass suspicious for adrenocortical carcinoma.

21-hydroxylase deficiency and 11β-hydroxylase deficiency. The block in 21-hydroxylase deficiency sits above 11-deoxycorticosterone, so no mineralocorticoid of any kind is made and the child salt-wastes. The block in 11β-hydroxylase deficiency sits one step below DOC, so DOC accumulates; it is a weak mineralocorticoid, but in that quantity it retains sodium, raises blood pressure, and wastes potassium. Both children virilize, because in both the accumulated precursor is shunted into the androgen branch.

Because secretion is episodic but metabolism is continuous. The tumour metabolizes catecholamines to metanephrines inside the tumour cells, around the clock, and leaks the metabolites steadily. So a catecholamine level drawn between paroxysms can be normal, whereas metanephrines stay elevated. That is what gives plasma free metanephrines their very high sensitivity — the property you want in a screening test for a tumour you cannot afford to miss.

MEN2A — hyperparathyroidism plus a pheochromocytoma, both from a RET mutation, with medullary thyroid carcinoma to be sought urgently. MEN1 includes hyperparathyroidism but not pheochromocytoma; MEN2B includes pheochromocytoma but not hyperparathyroidism. Before any operation the pheochromocytoma must be diagnosed biochemically and the patient α-blocked and volume-repleted. Anesthesia in an unprepared patient can precipitate a fatal hypertensive crisis, and the thyroid surgery this patient will eventually need must wait behind the adrenal.
