Study guide · Autoimmune diabetes · 7 Aug 2026

T1DM

One causal chain runs the whole page: autoimmune beta-cell destruction → absolute insulin deficiency → catabolism and hyperglycemia → ketogenesis → DKA. Every symptom, every lab value, and every complication on this page is a consequence of one missing hormone.

Insulin, glucagon, and the consequence of absolute deficiency

Glucose homeostasis is a balance of power between insulin, the anabolic storage signal, and glucagon and the other counter-regulatory hormones, which mobilize fuel. Insulin is the only hormone that lowers glucose; several act to raise it. Lose insulin entirely and the balance does not merely tilt — one side of it disappears, and the body behaves as though it is starving in the presence of abundant fuel.

Notice that the classic triad falls straight out of it: polyuria from the osmotic diuresis, polydipsia from the resulting dehydration, and polyphagia with weight loss from cells that cannot access glucose while fat and protein are being dismantled. Weight loss despite eating more is not a paradox — it is the whole mechanism in one sign.

Why gestational diabetes matters after the pregnancy ends

For the mother: gestational diabetes signals a substantial future risk of T2DM — which is why the 6-to-12-week postpartum test exists and why it should not be skipped. For the child: prematurity, birth defects, neonatal hypoglycemia, being large for gestational age, and metabolic syndrome in adolescence. One pregnancy generates two long-term risk trajectories.

RecognizeWhy it happens, how it presents, and what the labs show

Autoimmune beta-cell destruction

The three-part formula worth memorizing

Genetically susceptible individuals + an environmental trigger = an immune-system disease that destroys beta cells. Note the emphasis: this is primarily an immune-system disease that happens to be diagnosed by a glucose level. On histology, a normal islet gives way to insulitis — and the alpha and delta cells are preserved while the beta cells are picked off. The selectivity is the point: this is a targeted immune attack, not general pancreatic damage.

Original schematic; axes are relative and unscaled. The clinically important shape: the autoimmune process runs silently long before anything is noticeable, because the surviving beta cells compensate. Glucose only rises — and then rises fast — once that reserve is exhausted. This is why a child can appear entirely well one month and present in ketoacidosis the next, and why autoantibodies can be positive years before diagnosis.

Genetic risk
The strongest named genetic associations are HLA-DR3 and HLA-DR4, which affect antigen presentation to T cells.
Proposed environmental triggers
Viral and other environmental exposures may contribute, but no single trigger is established.

Clinical presentation

The three Ps
Polyuria — glucose exceeds the renal threshold and drags water with it. Polydipsia — thirst chasing that fluid loss. Polyphagia — hunger, because the cells cannot access the fuel circulating past them. In children, add nocturia and new nocturnal enuresis: a previously dry child wetting the bed again is a classic and easily dismissed presentation.
What makes it T1DM rather than T2DM on the history
Weight loss despite increased appetite, over weeks rather than years, in someone without an insulin-resistance phenotype. Loose clothing and a tightened belt are the details students overlook. A short, catabolic history points to absolute deficiency; an incidental A1c in someone with obesity points elsewhere.

The confirmation rule that questions are built on

Asymptomatic patients require two abnormal results, on subsequent days, to confirm the diagnosis. A single abnormal value in someone with no symptoms is not a diagnosis. The exception is the symptomatic patient with a random glucose above 199 — there, symptoms plus the value are enough, and the reason is obvious once you think about it: someone with classic symptoms and a glucose of 350 does not need a repeat test tomorrow, they need treatment today.

Autoantibodies — is it autoimmune?
GAD-65, ICA-512 (IA-2), and insulin autoantibodies (IAA). Their presence supports T1DM. Note the direction of the inference: they identify the mechanism, not the severity.
C-peptide — is there any endogenous insulin?
C-peptide is cleaved from proinsulin in a one-to-one ratio with insulin, so it measures what the pancreas is making. Low in T1DM (the beta cells are gone) and normal or high in T2DM (they are working hard against resistance). Critically, C-peptide is not present in injected insulin — so it is also the test that distinguishes endogenous hyperinsulinism from someone who has taken insulin.

The pancreas makes proinsulin and cleaves it into insulin and C-peptide in equal amounts, so anything the pancreas secretes raises both together. Manufactured insulin contains no C-peptide.

So: high insulin with high C-peptide = endogenous overproduction — an insulinoma, or a drug that pushes the beta cell to secrete, such as a sulfonylurea. High insulin with suppressed C-peptide = the insulin came from outside the body. The same logic makes C-peptide a marker of residual beta-cell function when classifying diabetes.

Management, and the acute complications short of DKA

The non-negotiable principle

T1DM requires insulin at all times — including when the patient is not eating, and including when they are unwell and eating nothing at all. Basal insulin is not "the insulin for food." Stopping it because someone is not eating is how a person ends up in ketoacidosis, because without any insulin the liver resumes ketogenesis regardless of intake.

Hypoglycemia

Why it is the limiting problem
Autonomic warning symptoms — sweating, tremor, palpitations, hunger, anxiety — normally give a person time to act. Hypoglycemia unawareness removes that warning, so the first sign becomes a neuroglycopenic one: altered mental status or seizures. Someone with unawareness is not less sick; they are less protected.
A pharmacology point worth carrying
Non-selective beta blockers blunt the adrenergic warning symptoms — the tremor and palpitations in particular — producing a drug-induced version of unawareness. Sweating is largely cholinergically mediated and tends to persist, which is why it is the symptom to ask about specifically in a patient on a beta blocker.

What the glucose did overnight — specifically, a reading in the early hours of the morning.

If the overnight trace ran steady or drifted upward, the morning high reflects the dawn phenomenon, and more overnight insulin is a reasonable direction. If the trace dipped low before rebounding, this is the Somogyi pattern, and increasing the evening dose would deepen a nocturnal hypoglycemia that is already happening while the patient is asleep and unable to notice it. The two look identical at 7 a.m. and call for opposite actions — which is the entire reason the pair is taught together.

Diabetic ketoacidosis

The acute complication the objectives ask you to explain mechanistically, recognize on labs, and manage in principle.

Scope, before we start

DKA is due to lack of insulin and is usually a feature of T1DM — but patients with T2DM can present with DKA too. Do not treat DKA as diagnostic of T1DM, and do not treat the presence of T2DM as reassurance against it.

Original diagram of the underlying mechanism: absence of intracellular insulin alters lipid metabolism, free fatty acids are shunted into ketone body formation, and ketones are acids — hence ketoacidosis.

Laboratory findings and physical signs

Diagnostic laboratory thresholds
Glucose typically above 250 mg/dL. Bicarbonate below 15 mEq/L. Increased anion gap, typically above 16. Ketones present in blood and urine.
Physical findings
Dehydration · Kussmaul respirations · a fruity odor on the breath from ketonemia · non-focal abdominal tenderness · nausea and vomiting · altered mental status. The abdominal pain is worth flagging — DKA is a well-known mimic of a surgical abdomen, and the pain typically resolves as the acidosis does.

The potassium paradox

  1. Total-body potassium is depleted by osmotic diuresis, even when serum potassium appears normal or high because insulin deficiency and acidosis shift potassium out of cells.
  2. Insulin drives potassium back into cells, so monitor closely and replace potassium during treatment.

Glaser N, Fritsch M, Priyambada L, et al. ISPAD Clinical Practice Consensus Guidelines 2022: Diabetic ketoacidosis and hyperglycemic hyperosmolar state. Pediatr Diabetes. 2022;23(7):835–856. doi:10.1111/pedi.13406

Management principles

Course principles only; clinical treatment follows an institutional protocol.

  1. Fluid first. These patients are volume depleted from the osmotic diuresis. Restoring circulating volume improves perfusion and, on its own, begins to lower the glucose by improving renal clearance of it.
  2. Insulin, to switch off ketogenesis. The purpose of the insulin is not primarily to lower the glucose — it is to stop lipolysis and ketone production and close the acidosis. This is why insulin continues even once the glucose has come down, with glucose added to the fluids to permit that.
  3. Potassium, anticipated rather than reacted to. See the paradox above. The expected fall is planned for.
  4. Monitor closely — glucose, electrolytes, acid–base status, and mental status. The anion gap closing is the marker that the underlying process is resolving; the glucose normalizing is not.
  5. Find and treat the precipitant. Infection, missed insulin, an interrupted pump, or a new diagnosis. A DKA that is treated without the trigger being identified tends to recur.
  6. Watch for cerebral edema throughout, particularly in children.

Cerebral edema

The complication that dominates DKA mortality

Cerebral edema is the complication that dominates mortality from DKA in children. It is a severe, life-threatening complication needing urgent treatment — typically mannitol or 3% saline.

Warning signs: altered mentation or a fluctuating level of consciousness · a sustained, unexpected deceleration of heart rate · incontinence · emesis · headache · lethargy · diastolic hypertension. Age under 5 years is a risk factor.

Two of those deserve emphasis because they run counter to intuition: a falling heart rate and a rising blood pressure in a patient who has been tachycardic and dehydrated look superficially like improvement. In this context they suggest rising intracranial pressure, and they are an emergency.
